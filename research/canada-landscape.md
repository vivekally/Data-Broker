# Canada holds the data, brokers nothing

**The central finding of this research is a negative one, and it is the most valuable thing in this report: there is almost no Canadian AI training-data broker market to compete in.** Across mining, oil and gas, forestry, agriculture, rail, ports, robotics, autonomous vehicles and healthcare, not one Canadian operator was found licensing its proprietary operational data to an AI developer as training data [U, searched across trade press, company releases and cluster announcements; absence of evidence, not verified absence]. No Canadian company sells AV perception data, dashcam video, teleoperation logs or robot demonstration data as a product [U, same caveat]. Canadian health data is effectively not brokerable for structural rather than legal reasons: the major stewards grant access to a secure environment rather than possession of data, and the country's first governance toolkit for hospital-to-industry data licensing shipped as a free public good in January 2026 [V, [INOVAIT](https://inovait.ca/data), 5 Jan 2026]. An industry directory sweep of roughly forty RL-environment vendors found zero Canadian headquarters [V, [RL List 2026](https://www.rl-list.com/)]. No Canadian pure-play AI data-supply company was found to have raised a venture round, while Toronto's Radical Ventures put its data-infrastructure money into a non-Canadian company [V, [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/investment-tracker/2026/prime-intellect-raises-130-million-to-help-companies-train-ai-agents/), 2026]. What Canada does have is a resource position rather than an intermediary position: one globally significant labelling operator in Vancouver, a deep bench of first-party sector-data holders in energy, geospatial and health, a credible privacy and de-identification layer, and a federal government spending public money to build the one deliberately AI-ready industrial corpus in the country. The gap between what Canada owns and what Canada sells is the whole story, and it is wide enough to be either the opportunity or the warning, depending on how you read the rest of this report.

## Two different companies are called Scale AI, and only one of them is Canadian

Before any figure in this report is read, this collision has to be cleared, because it corrupts search results, database listings and market maps alike.

**Scale AI (Canada)** is properly *Canada's AI-Powered Supply Chains Cluster*, a Montréal-headquartered federal Global Innovation Cluster launched in 2018 at `scaleai.ca`. It is a grant-maker. It reimburses up to 40% of eligible project expenses, and up to 50% for qualifying Québec expenditures, on 12 to 18 month AI-adoption projects [V, [scaleai.ca projects](https://www.scaleai.ca/en/projects/)]. It is funded with **C$284M from the Government of Canada and C$53M from the Government of Québec**, matched by private contributions [V, [scaleai.ca](https://www.scaleai.ca/about-us/)]; the launch announcement in 2018 was framed as **CAD 230 million in matched federal funding** [V, [ISED](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/global-innovation-clusters/en/canadas-ai-powered-supply-chains-cluster-scale-ai); [Research Money](https://researchmoneyinc.com/article/trudeau-announces-230-million-for-montreal-artificial-intelligence-supercluster-scale-ai)].

**Scale AI, Inc.** is a San Francisco data-labelling and RL-environment company at `scale.com`, with roughly 300,000 contractors, US$870M of 2024 revenue, and a US$29B valuation after Meta bought 49% of it for US$14.3B in June 2025 [V, [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/scale-ai/); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/richardnieva/2026/05/14/scale-meta-deal/), 14 May 2026].

**They have no relationship whatsoever.** Every search for "Scale AI Canada" returns the cluster. A Tracxn "investments by Scale AI" listing surfaced during this research and almost certainly conflates the two [I, from the incompatible business models]. Throughout this report the Montréal cluster is written as **Scale AI (Canada)** and the US company as **Scale AI Inc.** There is one place where both appear in the same sentence and it matters: **Scale AI Inc. supplied the lidar ground-truth annotations for the Canadian Adverse Driving Conditions dataset** produced by the University of Waterloo and the University of Toronto [V, [arXiv:2001.10117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10117)]. That is a US vendor selling services *into* a Canadian academic dataset, which is exactly the direction of flow this report keeps finding.

## Eleven supply categories, and Canada is absent from the three that pay most

The nine candidate categories in the original brief largely hold, but two need splitting. "Data brokers and marketplaces" is not one category in Canada; it is two economically unrelated businesses, legacy consumer data brokers whose buyer is an advertiser versus AI-training dataset marketplaces whose buyer is a model lab, and Canada has real depth in the first and almost nothing in the second. Synthetic data likewise splits cleanly into privacy-preserving record synthesis and simulation or world-model synthesis, which share a name and share nothing else. Published commercial market maps are cruder: the most common vendor framing is a four-bucket one of marketplaces, labelling services, custom sourcing partners and synthetic data generators [V, [forage.ai](https://forage.ai/blog/ai-training-data-providers/), 2026, itself a vendor in the category]. Conventional analyst segmentation cuts by modality and end-use industry, which is useless for a market map because a labelling shop and a synthetic-data vendor both land under "healthcare, text" [I].

The useful discriminator is the **unit of value charged**, because it differs sharply by category and it determines who the buyer is.

| # | Category | Unit of value | Typical buyer | Canadian presence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Data labelling, annotation and collection services | The label, at industrial volume | Frontier labs, AV programmes, search and ads teams | **Strong, one firm.** TELUS Digital (Vancouver); LXT (Toronto) |
| B | Expert / human-data networks ("data foundries") | The expert hour | Frontier labs and neolabs almost exclusively | **None independent.** Only TELUS Digital's own AI Community |
| C | RL environment and eval builders | The environment plus its verifier | Frontier labs almost exclusively | **Zero.** Verified absence across ~40 vendors |
| D1 | Legacy consumer and commercial data brokers | The audience segment or record | Marketers, retailers, lenders. **Not labs** | **Strong, wrong buyer.** Environics Analytics (Bell-owned) |
| D2 | AI-training dataset marketplaces | The licensed asset-hour, with rev-share | Labs and neolabs | **One new, narrow entrant.** RHEI (Vancouver) |
| E1 | Privacy-preserving synthetic record generation | The synthetic record set, or a seat | Pharma, payers, health systems, regulators | **Real origin, now foreign-owned.** Replica Analytics (Ottawa, Aetion) |
| E2 | Simulation / world-model synthetic data | Internal; not merchant-sold | AV and robotics developers, usually in-house | **Strong but captive.** Waabi, Sanctuary AI |
| F | Data clearance, licensing and rights intermediaries | Commission on the licensed asset | Labs, via rightsholders | **One entrant; the collective has not entered.** RHEI; Access Copyright absent |
| G | Sector data holders monetising first-party data | The observation, the facility-month, the subscription seat | Operators, regulators, insurers, and increasingly labs | **The deepest Canadian bench.** GHGSat, Ecopia, MDA, geoLOGIC, SkyWatch, Pentavere |
| H | De-identification, pipeline and federated-learning tooling | The seat or deployment | Any enterprise with regulated data | **Real.** Private AI (Toronto) |
| I | Consent / permissioned-data platforms | The consenting individual, as a subscription | Advertisers and data platforms today; labs aspirationally | **One public company, struggling.** Reklaim (Toronto) |

Categories A and B are converging at the vendor level and should not be treated as competing firms: Scale AI Inc., Surge AI, Mercor and Turing all appear as both "data giants" in the RL-environment taxonomy and as human-data vendors, and TELUS Digital markets both bulk annotation and 20+ expert domains off one contributor pool [I, from [RL List](https://www.rl-list.com/) and [telusdigital.com](https://www.telusdigital.com/solutions/data-for-ai-training)].

**The three clean negatives are C, B and D2.** The RL-environment negative is the most defensible finding in this research, because it rests on an industry-maintained directory sweeping the whole vendor population and finding roughly **28 companies in the San Francisco Bay Area, 7 in New York**, plus Gray Swan AI in Pittsburgh, Huzzle Labs in London and Andromede in Lausanne, and **zero Canadian headquarters** [V, [RL List 2026](https://www.rl-list.com/)]. The same sweep noted that some of those teams have University of Waterloo alumni, which makes this a talent-export finding rather than a market-presence finding [V, same source; I on the interpretation]. For category B, the named players across every source reached are Scale AI Inc., Surge AI, Mercor, Turing and Centific, all US, and the contributor-side platforms Canadians can join are Mercor, DataAnnotation.tech, Outlier and Alignerr, all US-operated [V, [Troveo](https://www.troveo.ai/resources/rl-environment-companies); [aigigjobs.com](https://www.aigigjobs.com/blog/alignerr-vs-mercor-2026)]. For D2, the named marketplace platforms are AWS Data Exchange, Databricks Marketplace, Scale AI Inc. and Hugging Face, plus Troveo (US) and Human Native AI (UK) [V, [marketintelo](https://marketintelo.com/report/dataset-licensing-for-ai-training-market); [Troveo](https://www.troveo.ai/resources/ai-data-licensing)].

There is a fourth, near-negative: **collective rights licensing**. Access Copyright is Canada's copyright collective for published text works, collecting from businesses, government, schools and libraries [V, [accesscopyright.ca](https://www.accesscopyright.ca/)]. No evidence was found that it has launched an AI training licence, while the US Copyright Clearance Center launched a collective AI licence in July 2024 [V, [Publishing Perspectives](https://publishingperspectives.com/2024/07/ccc-announces-a-collective-licensing-subscription-for-ai-systems/)] [U on whether an Access Copyright product exists and was simply not found; three sources were checked and none surfaced one].

The reason categories B and C are empty in Canada is the same reason, and it is structural rather than accidental. Winning in the environment market means being embedded in a lab's research loop: "Frontier labs gravitate toward teams they trust and treat as extensions of their own research organizations, and winning teams will look like thought partners embedded with frontier labs rather than traditional tooling vendors" [V, [Wing VC](https://www.wing.vc/content/who-will-win-the-rl-environment-market--and-why)]. There is no frontier lab in Canada of the relevant scale to embed inside, and the nearest analogue, Cohere, is a buyer rather than a seller in this market, and even that is unestablished (see the demand section).

One caution on sizing before moving on. Anthropic was reported to have discussed spending **over US$1 billion on RL environments over the following year** [V that this was reported, [The Information](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/state-of-rl-envs) via Epoch AI, Sept 2025 report cited Jan 2026] [U on the underlying figure; it is a second-hand characterisation of private spend, and the original report is roughly 12 months old, so treat as **STALE**]. Set against a measured total AI training-dataset market of **US$3.19B in 2025 rising to US$3.87B in 2026** [V, [The Business Research Company](https://www.thebusinessresearchcompany.com/report/ai-training-dataset-global-market-report)], a single lab's environment budget would be a material fraction of the entire measured market. Either the market sizings exclude RL environments or they are badly stale [I]. Do not reconcile these figures; the discrepancy is itself the finding.

## One giant, a resource bench, and a great deal of foreign ownership

Canada's position in this market is a **resource position, not an intermediary position**. Of the eleven categories above, Canadian firms cluster in G (own the data), E2 (generate it via simulation), E1 and H (make it safe to use), and A (process it at industrial scale). The intermediary categories that capture the most frontier-lab spend per head are essentially absent [I, from the category-by-category evidence].

Three of the four Canadian warehouse and mobile-robotics data holders identified are now under foreign ownership through M&A or insolvency [I]. **Insolvency is quietly becoming a data-transfer channel**, and it is a real and under-examined route by which Canadian data leaves Canadian hands [I, from the 2025 Attabotics and LeddarTech proceedings].

### Global players with Canadian operations or Canadian data generation

**Gatik** is the clearest live case. It is a US company [V, [Businesswire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260127742958/en/Gatik-Becomes-First-U.S.-Company-to-Operate-Fully-Driverless-Trucks-at-Scale-for-Commercial-Deliveries), 27 Jan 2026] operating a five-year expansion agreement with Loblaw that puts **20 autonomous trucks across the GTA distribution network by end of 2025 and a further 30 by end of 2026, for 50 trucks operating daily in Greater Toronto** [V, [Loblaw](https://www.loblaw.ca/en/gatik-and-loblaw-ink-5-year-expansion-deal-to-scale-ai-powered-autonomous-trucking-solution-across-regional-distribution-networks-in-the-greater-toronto-area/)]. In January 2026 its contracts were reported at **US$600M** [V, [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/alanohnsman/2026/01/28/hundreds-of-gatik-robot-delivery-trucks-headed-for-us-roads/)]. **Canadian-origin road data, including the winter data discussed below, is accumulating on a US company's servers with no Canadian entity acquiring rights to it, and no data sale is taking place** [I].

**IQVIA** is the closest thing to a commercial health-data intermediary operating in Canada, but it sits in the pharma real-world-evidence channel sourced from pharmacy, claims and EMR data rather than the single-payer channel [I]. It markets Canada as "an attractive location for Real World Evidence studies," citing **15+ years of ICD-10 use in Canadian hospital and emergency settings** [V, [IQVIA](https://www.iqvia.com/blogs/2025/01/exploring-real-world-data-in-canada), January 2025, **20 months old**]. Its global portfolio claim of "over 1.2 billion de-identified patient records" is a company self-claim with no disclosed Canadian share [V that the claim was made, iqvia.com] [U].

**LiveRamp** reaches the Canadian market through Bell-owned Environics rather than directly [V, [DMN](https://dmn.ca/bell-media-implementing-liveramps-authenticated-traffic-solution/)]. **Welocalize / Welo Global** recruits "AI Data Annotator, French (Canada)" roles, which is direct evidence that Canadian-French linguistic data collection is being commissioned by a foreign localisation vendor [V that the posting exists, [Lever](https://jobs.lever.co/weloglobal/a4eea322-dd9f-49ef-8b5b-df7620786b91); I on the interpretation]. And the **US gig platforms** (Mercor, Outlier, DataAnnotation, Alignerr) recruit Canadian contractors directly with no Canadian legal entity [V that the claim was published, [theaiemploymentagency.com](https://theaiemploymentagency.com/blog/mercor-vs-dataannotation-vs-outlier)] [U, affiliate blog; no primary platform terms were read].

That last point produces one of the more interesting inferences in this research. **Canada's participation in the global human-data supply chain is as an individual labour pool, not as a corporate node** [I]. And because the rates available to Canadians through those platforms (Mercor at US$40-100+/hr, DataAnnotation at US$20-65/hr, Outlier at US$14-30/hr generalist) sit in the *high* band of the global annotation workforce [V that the ranges were published, [aigigjobs.com](https://www.aigigjobs.com/platforms/mercor)] [U on accuracy; treat the ordering as more reliable than the numbers], **Canada is structurally uncompetitive for volume labelling and potentially competitive for expert data** [I].

### The public and quasi-public holders, which are the most consequential Canadian assets

Four institutions hold more relevant data than any Canadian company, and none of them is a seller.

**ICES** (Ontario), founded 1992, is a prescribed entity under PHIPA with access to administrative health records covering **13.4 million people** [V, [ices.on.ca](https://www.ices.on.ca/); [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7477779/)]. Its inventory "is stored on servers housed within a closed computing system at ICES Central," scientists "must apply for and justify each dataset requested," and the main external pathway is the Applied Health Research Question model in which ICES analysts run the query and return results [V, [ices.on.ca](https://www.ices.on.ca/data-and-privacy/working-with-ices-data)]. **ICES is effectively closed to data egress** [I].

**CIHI** holds national record-level data behind a Secure Access Environment and runs an explicit two-tier commercial price list: data preparation at **$190/hour (Price A) versus $380/hour (Price B)**, and SAE annual access at **$3,325 versus $6,650 for up to three users**, where Price B covers "private commercial operations, software vendors, consultants" and foreign clients [V, [CIHI](https://www.cihi.ca/en/access-data-and-reports/make-a-data-request), fetched Sept 2026; fee-table vintage not dated on the page]. This is the single clearest priced commercial route into Canadian health data, and it is **a services fee, not a data licence**: it is denominated in analyst hours and per-user seats rather than dataset scope, term or field of use, which is the economic signature of cost recovery [I]. CIHI's public page contains no explicit restriction on AI training [V by absence] but that is an absence, not a permission, and the SAE Agreement text was not retrieved [U].

**Population Data BC** holds demographic, health, education, social services, justice, workers' compensation and ICBC data, applies the Five Safes framework, and reports managing **345 data access requests or projects** at various stages [V, [popdata.bc.ca](https://popdata.bc.ca/data)]. Whether private commercial entities are eligible at all in BC was not established [U].

**VITAL** is the one to watch. Based at St. Michael's Hospital within Unity Health Toronto and rooted in the GEMINI network founded in 2014 by Dr. Fahad Razak and Dr. Amol Verma, it will connect **more than 160 hospitals across Ontario, Alberta and Quebec, covering over 20 million Canadians**, described as the largest hospital data network in Canada [V, search-snippet sourcing citing unityhealth.to and CBC, June 2026; **weaker than a fetched page**]. It received **$100 million in federal funding** under the "AI for All" strategy announced in June 2026, on top of $110M in prior federal, provincial and institutional funding, for **over $210 million total** [V, same snippet sourcing]. **VITAL is the first Canadian institution designed from the outset as an AI-training-data supply layer rather than a research-access layer** [I, from its framing as giving health AI developers "a national foundation to build on" and its placement inside a federal AI strategy rather than a health-research program]. **Its actual industry access terms, pricing and IP allocation are unknown, and this is the single highest-value open question in the entire body of research** [U].

Alongside these sits the **Canadian Digital Core Library (CDCL)**, the most deliberate attempt anywhere in Canada to manufacture an AI-ready industrial corpus. NRCan is investing **$40M over two years starting in 2026-27**, with **$15M allocated to the Creative Destruction Lab** to build the national platform and **$2.16M to support Saskatchewan's digital core library**; **10 provinces and territories have signed participation agreements**; and **core scanning began on 14 September 2026 in Calgary**, starting with Northwest Territories samples [V, [Newswire / Government of Canada](https://www.newswire.ca/news-releases/canada-advances-the-canadian-digital-core-library-with-new-funding-and-the-start-of-core-scanning-807070186.html), Sept 2026]. NRCan is separately offering **up to $5 million apiece** for provincial and territorial geoscience data projects until **31 March 2028** [V, [Nunavut News](https://nunavutnews.com/2026/09/21/new-ai-ready-mine-drill-core-library-will-unlock-canadas-resource-potential-says-federal-government/), 21 Sept 2026]. Crucially, "established core-scanning providers are invited to contribute services and resulting data **in kind**," which is the closest thing in Canada to an industrial data-pooling mechanism [V, Newswire, Sept 2026; I on the characterisation]. Six mining majors are reported to be participating: **Agnico Eagle, Anglo American, BHP, Teck, Vale and Hudbay** [V, [Giant](https://www.begiant.ca/stories/ideas/canadian-digital-core-library-critical-minerals), Aug 2026] [I, single secondary source; NRCan's own September release does not name them, so treat participation as reported rather than contractual]. **Whether CDCL data will be open-licence or commercially licensed is not stated in any source found, and it is the single most commercially important unanswered question about the asset** [U].

## The demand side, where the honest answer is mostly nobody

This is the section that will disappoint anyone hoping for a clean buyer map, and the disappointment is the finding.

**Not one Canadian company was established as selling training data to a named frontier lab.** Not in industrial data, not in robotics or AV, not in healthcare [U, across three separate research threads, each with incomplete searches acknowledged]. The closest to a positive signal is TELUS: after taking TELUS Digital private, TELUS reported that the "Digital Solutions pipeline reaccelerated in Q4 [2025], driving 17% YoY revenue growth, landing new AI data projects with hyperscalers" [V, [TELUS FY2025 results](https://www.telus.com/en/about/news-and-events/media-releases/telus-reports-strong-and-industry-leading-operational-and-financial-results-for-the-fourth-quarter-and-full-year-2025), Feb 2026]. The hyperscalers are unnamed, and **TELUS does not break out AI Data Solutions revenue separately** [V, absence confirmed in the filing summary]. The privatization on 31 October 2025 removed the only public disclosure window into the largest Canadian player in this market, which means any future sizing of the Canadian category will be blind to its largest component [I].

Where Canadian data does have paying buyers, those buyers are **operators, regulators, insurers and pharma sponsors, not model labs**. GHGSat sells methane observations to ExxonMobil and Aramco. MDA sells EO data to nine early customers and to NASA. geoLOGIC sells subscription access to well data to the oil and gas industry. Pentavere sells real-world evidence extraction to Takeda Canada and to the Canadian Personalized Health Innovation Network. Environics sells segmentation to marketers. Reklaim sells consented consumer data to "brands & agencies, platforms, and data companies." Rithmik sells per-asset monitoring subscriptions to mine operators. **Canadian rail and port operational data is being consumed by AI vendors under service contracts (Duos, Informatica, Deloitte with AWS, Mely.ai, IVADO Labs) rather than sold by the asset owners: in every arrangement found, the asset owner is the paying customer, not the seller** [I, from the structure of every rail and port arrangement identified].

Three specific demand-side observations are worth carrying forward because they are load-bearing.

**First, the one non-content operational archive price point found anywhere.** Google reportedly bid **US$10 million for a Spirit Airlines operational archive** [V, [Troveo](https://www.troveo.ai/resources/ai-data-licensing), 23 July 2026; single-source and unusual]. That is the only evidence located that a frontier-lab-class buyer will price a non-media operational dataset at all, and it is worth more attention than its single-source status suggests, because it is the closest analogue to what a Canadian industrial data asset would be selling.

**Second, Canada's nearest frontier lab does not appear to buy.** Cohere is co-headquartered in Toronto and San Francisco and staffs a standing bench of domain annotators on its own payroll: Data Annotation Specialist roles across Investment Banking and Private Equity, Safety, Competitive Coding, Science, Data Science and Generalist, structured as "part-time contract positions requiring **16 hours per week at a $30/hour contract rate**" [V, [Cohere Ashby board](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/cohere), 29 Sept 2026]. No supplier-facing intake path, no data-partnerships team title, and no reported data-licensing deal were found [U, a genuine void rather than a negative finding]. **Cohere's data posture looks insourced rather than procured, which is a meaningfully different buying profile from US labs that route heavy volume through Surge and Mercor** [I]. For a Canadian supplier this is the awkward finding: the nearest lab is the one least evidenced to buy from third parties, and its published $30/hour internal reference rate is a low bar for a fully-loaded Canadian vendor rate to beat [I].

**Third, and most underappreciated: AI scribes are probably the largest live channel by which Canadian clinical data reaches commercial AI training.** Ambient listening tools capture confidential health information, explicit patient consent is required, and "when patients consent, they agree their data can be used to train the AI model" [V, [Policy Options](https://policyoptions.irpp.org/2026/04/ai-scribes-health-care-canada-privacy-safety-risks), April 2026]. This is a consent-based, per-encounter flow that bypasses the institutional licensing problem entirely [I]. It merits dedicated follow-up, because it means the formal licensing debate is happening while the informal channel is already operating.

For completeness on the buyers who exist but cannot transact: Canada has **more than 80 companies working with health data and AI** to improve triaging, remote monitoring and diagnostics [V, search snippet citing techforum.ca coverage of the VITAL announcement, June 2026]. The claim that these 80-plus companies "now have access to a national-scale dataset" through VITAL is promotional framing and the access terms are unspecified; it is unlikely that 80 companies had executed data agreements as of mid-2026 [I]. What they actually encounter is "limited, and often dysfunctional, access to data" [V, Boyer & Roifman, *Healthcare Management Forum*, doi 10.1177/08404704261454317, 2026, via search snippet], and a hospital default of refusal: "industry is often regarded with suspicion or distrust, and in such scenarios, **not sharing data can become a default**" [V, [Torys Q&A with Phil Boyer](https://www.torys.com/), fetched Sept 2026]. **The binding constraint on the buy side is counterparty willingness and transaction cost, not price.** CIHI's Price B at $380/hour and $6,650/year for three SAE seats is trivially affordable for any funded AI company, and the sector still reports dysfunctional access. If price were the constraint, that tier would have solved it [I].

This is the most operationally useful section in the report, and it is also the best-evidenced, because frontier labs publish detailed job postings for their data-buying roles even when they publish no supplier intake path at all. **Job postings are the single best free intelligence source for a supplier**, because they state the team name, the reporting line, the workflow and the evaluation criteria the buyer will apply [I, from the Anthropic, Reflection AI, micro1 and Cohere postings recovered versus the one intake form found across four labs].

The practical conclusion is that there are effectively **two self-serve entry points for a supplier with no warm relationship: the OpenAI form, and subcontracting under an intermediary that already holds lab contracts.** Epoch AI categorises RL-environment sellers into four buckets, one of which is "traditional data providers (Mercor, Surge, Handshake, Turing)" already holding lab relationships [V, [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/state-of-rl-envs), 12 January 2026]. Surge AI's growth was "mainly driven by a group of ~12 frontier AI labs" [V, [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/surge-ai/) and Contrary Research profiles; analyst aggregators, treat the customer list as reported rather than confirmed]. **With roughly a dozen labs accounting for most of the demand, and each intermediary already holding several of those accounts, a new supplier's fastest path to lab revenue is subcontracting under an incumbent rather than winning a direct MSA** [I]. Curated directories exist that function as matchmaking (Pavlov's List, RL List), and HUD reports "over 50 businesses" using its platform to build environments and sell them to labs [V as HUD's own claim, surfaced 29 Sept 2026] [U on effectiveness as a channel].

### Who owns the money

The buying function splits across at least three distinct owners, and a supplier must satisfy all three. At Anthropic all three exist as separately titled, separately paid roles, which is the strongest available evidence that frontier-lab data buying has professionalised into a real procurement function with the standard enterprise gates [I].

The **Procurement Operations Business Partner, R&D Operations** sits in Finance Operations at **US$190,000 to US$230,000 base**, and "owns the path from 'I need to buy something' to a signed agreement, an accurate purchase order, and clean spend data at month-end." Its preferred qualification is, verbatim: *"Sourced human data, data labeling, annotation, or evaluation vendors (or comparable BPO and outsourced operations) with a point of view on where that market is heading"* [V, Anthropic Greenhouse posting 5357933008, live 29 Sept 2026]. **Human-data vendor sourcing sits inside Anthropic's Finance procurement function, not only in research.**

The **Data Operations Lead, Partnerships** sits in Sales & Business Development at **US$230,000 to US$300,000 plus equity**, and its remit is to "develop data standardization and consumption standards aligned with Product & Engineering needs," "collaborate with internal and external partners to acquire strategically useful data," and "oversee implementation of data partnerships and monitor delivery metrics" [V, Anthropic posting via the Menlo Ventures job board] [**STALE**: the posting is 6+ months old and closed; the role may have been filled or restructured]. Note the pay gradient: **the acquisition role out-earns the procurement operations role at the same company, so sourcing the data is valued above processing the purchase** [I].

At smaller labs the three functions collapse into one. Reflection AI's *Data Partnerships & Initiatives Manager*, a listing updated on the research date itself, is asked to "own new initiatives and partnerships end-to-end: evaluating suppliers, negotiating agreements, and verifying deliverables," to work with researchers to establish quality requirements and sourcing pipelines, and requires 6+ years in business operations, strategy, VC, private equity or consulting plus SQL, Python, Tableau and Excel [V, Simplify Jobs posting, 29 Sept 2026]. **One person to convince, but that person applies a diligence-style evaluation** [I]. No named individuals were identified for any lab, deliberately not guessed [U], and who holds actual budget authority as opposed to running the process was not established anywhere [U].

### The gate sequence, verbatim from the buyer

Anthropic's procurement posting names the workflow in writing. Before a purchase request can move at all, the procurement partner must "determine what each request requires, whether that is a statement of work, an order form, an amendment, **a security or privacy review, or a third party risk assessment**." Then: "launch and track contract review with Legal, keep it moving, and confirm the executed agreement supports the purchase order," and structure purchase orders "matching vendor billing models and accounting needs with correct GL coding" [V, Anthropic Greenhouse posting 5357933008, verbatim, live 29 Sept 2026].

The most commercially useful sentence in the entire body of research is this one: the same role is "the dedicated operations partner for our research services vendor portfolio, where engagements are structured as **master agreements with frequent work orders and scope changes**" [V, same posting, verbatim]. **The instrument for anything resembling ongoing human data or RL environments is MSA plus SOW or work order, not a one-shot licence, and scope creep is written into the buyer's job description as the normal state. A supplier that prices a fixed scope tightly is mismatched to how the buyer operates** [I].

Two review tracks that have nothing to do with data quality run in parallel and both are named as blocking: **security or privacy review, and third-party risk assessment** [I, from the verbatim text]. And because "verifying deliverables" appears as an explicit named responsibility alongside "delivery metrics," **held-back acceptance testing against research-defined quality requirements should be assumed standard** [I].

Three things the evidence does *not* support and which should not be assumed: no source describes a formal pilot or trial-dataset stage with a named duration or sample size at any frontier lab [U]; no source describes evaluation against held-out tasks as a contractual acceptance criterion [U]; and **no typical cycle time from first contact to signature was found for any lab** [U, do not put a number here].

### Contract shapes and what things cost

Two contract families exist and they should not be blended.

**Family A is content and archive licensing**, with the structures being flat fee (one-time or fixed multi-year), recurring annual licence, usage-based, revenue share, or equity hybrid [V, [Troveo](https://www.troveo.ai/resources/ai-data-licensing), 23 July 2026; Troveo is a marketplace and therefore an interested party]. **Almost every large figure in circulation is stale**, which matters enormously: there is no reliable 2026 public comparable for a new content licence [I, from the dates on every figure recovered].

**Family B is human data and RL environments**, priced per task, per environment, or per hour of expert time under a master agreement. The anchor source here is Epoch AI's FAQ of **12 January 2026**, built on interviews with **18 stakeholders** across RL environment startups, neolabs and frontier labs [V, [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/state-of-rl-envs)]. It was 8.5 months old at the research date, inside the freshness window but in the fastest-moving segment of the market.

At US$200 to US$2,000 per task, a seven-figure quarter implies roughly **500 to 5,000 tasks per quarter** per contract; Epoch does not state this and the ranges are wide, so treat it strictly as an order-of-magnitude sanity check [I]. More usefully, the 4-5x exclusivity multiple sets a clean strategic fork: **one exclusive lab relationship is worth roughly the same as four to five non-exclusive ones, so exclusivity is close to economically neutral at the point of sale, and the real decision is about risk concentration and optionality rather than revenue** [I]. Non-exclusive multi-lab resale is a normal and permitted structure, not a workaround [V, Epoch AI]. In content licensing the pattern inverts: exclusivity is described as "the biggest single lever on price" with **field-of-use exclusivity, exclusive for one type of model or application, named as a common compromise**, but with no public multiple [V, practitioner sources; "several times" is not quantified and should not be converted into a number]. **Do not extend the 4-5x figure beyond RL environments** [U]. Typical exclusivity term length was not found for any deal [U].

Four things must not be asserted, because no source supports them: any per-record price, any revenue-share percentage, any deal cycle time, and any claim that Cohere buys third-party training data [all U].

### The four gates that kill deals

**Gate one is research-side quality fit**, established before sourcing begins through the buyer's own quality requirements [V, Reflection AI posting].

**Gate two is procedural and named by the buyer in writing**: security or privacy review, and third-party risk assessment, both of which block a request from moving at all [V, Anthropic Greenhouse posting 5357933008]. No lab-published trust and security requirements document for data suppliers was located, for any lab, so a supplier cannot currently know in advance what SOC 2, pen-test or subprocessor standard they will be held to [U].

**Gate three is chain of title**, and Canada has the market's clearest live demonstration. In **Advance Local Media LLC v. Cohere Inc.**, case no. 1:25-cv-01305 in the Southern District of New York, filed February 2025 by fourteen news and magazine publishers including Forbes, Condé Nast, the Los Angeles Times and The Atlantic, **Judge McMahon denied Cohere's partial motion to dismiss on 13 November 2025**, finding that publishers plausibly stated claims for direct copyright infringement including for "substitutive summaries" generated by Cohere's models, for secondary infringement on inducement and contributory theories, and for trademark infringement and false designation of origin based on display of plaintiffs' marks alongside hallucinated articles. The court rejected Cohere's argument that summaries differed in tone, style, length and sentence structure, noting some outputs "were nearly identical to the underlying work, lifting several paragraphs in their entirety," and declined to adopt a bright-line test [V, [BakerHostetler case tracker](https://www.bakerlaw.com/); Loeb & Loeb; Copyright Lately; CourtListener docket]. **The ruling is ~10.5 months old and the case has almost certainly progressed; check for post-November-2025 developments before acting on this** [flagged]. The straightforward implication: **a Canadian AI company is being sued in a US court over training data, so Canadian domicile provides no meaningful shelter from US copyright exposure** [I].

The standard clause set a supplier will negotiate against covers permitted uses, covered models and successors, term and deletion obligations, **survival of trained models**, exclusivity and field of use, **rights and provenance warranties**, indemnification and its cap, privacy and de-identification standards, audit and reporting rights, output restrictions, payment structure and confidentiality [V, practitioner write-ups from Terms.Law, Galkin Law and DarrowEverett; law-firm marketing content, credible on structure, not empirical on frequency]. Two of these are decisive. **"Survival of trained models"** determines whether the model trained on the data survives termination of the licence, which is likely the single most contested clause: a licence that does not survive is worth far less to the lab, while one that does survive means the supplier has effectively sold the asset once and permanently [I, not stated directly by any source]. And **the indemnity** is the practical deal-killer for a small supplier: "the negotiating leverage point is whether the vendor can represent and warrant that its training data was lawfully obtained and adequately licensed, with vendors who cannot make that warranty required to carry **IP insurance naming the enterprise as an additional insured**" [V, practitioner sources]. An uncapped or high-cap IP indemnity, or an IP insurance requirement, can exceed the economics of a small deal entirely [I]. A workable compromise practitioners describe is "a summary provenance file at licence date, with a fuller record made available on request in the event of a third-party claim" [V, same].

**Gate four is competitive and ownership conflict**, and the Scale AI Inc. episode is the natural experiment. "Within weeks of Meta's investment closing, Google, OpenAI, and xAI all cut or significantly reduced their contracts with Scale AI, unwilling to route their proprietary training data and evaluation pipelines through a vendor now half-owned by a direct competitor. That business didn't disappear, it moved to rivals Mercor and Surge AI" [V, analyst write-ups of the June 2025 Meta transaction; **STALE as an event at ~15 months, but the structural lesson stands**]. **Three labs exited a functioning vendor relationship within weeks purely on ownership grounds, which makes cap-table conflict a first-order procurement gate independent of price or quality** [I].

### What the intermediaries reveal about what labs actually want

The sell-side motion of the three biggest intermediaries has converged on the same thesis, and it is the clearest available read on demand: **labs are not buying labels, they are buying scarce human expertise applied to tasks, at a verified quality bar, that they cannot obtain by contracting with the companies that own the workflows.**

Mercor's co-founder Brendan Foody put the premise directly: enterprise customers "don't want to give them data to automate large portions of their value chains," so labs "need to hire contractors who previously worked at those companies, understand those workflows, and are willing to train models to automate them." He added: "There is an argument that Goldman Sachs doesn't love the idea of having models that are able to automate their value chain" [V, [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/), 29 Oct 2025, **11 months old**]. What labs buy through Mercor is experts who "fill out forms and write reports for AI training," and in some cases contractors who "authorize access to production codebases for 'AI evaluations' or model training" [V, same]. **That last detail is a chain-of-title exposure sitting inside the fastest-growing segment of the market: labs are acquiring workflow knowledge, and sometimes production-codebase access, from individuals rather than from the entities that own it** [I; no source identifies this as a live legal problem yet, U].

Surge positions on quality rather than throughput, focusing on "PhD-level labelers and subject matter experts" [V, review aggregators]. Epoch's four-bucket seller taxonomy names the structural ceiling explicitly: specialised RL-environment startups; traditional data providers (Mercor, Surge, Handshake, Turing); **in-house teams at model developers (Anthropic, xAI, Cursor)**; and product companies licensing their own software as environments (Benchling with Anthropic, Shopify and Stripe with OpenAI) [V, Epoch AI, 12 Jan 2026]. **Because in-house teams appear as a peer category to vendors rather than as customers, every external supplier competes against an internal build decision on every work order** [I].

### Canada-specific friction in the buying workflow

Three frictions, and only the first is well evidenced.

The **CLOUD Act conflict** is real and specific: "Canadian subsidiaries of US technology companies remain subject to CLOUD Act disclosure requirements, creating direct tension with PIPEDA's expectation of comparable protection in cross-border transfers and with Law 25 compliance obligations" [V, Canadian data-sovereignty write-ups, 2026; secondary sources, legally plausible and consistent across results]. Critically, **this friction is asymmetric by data type**. A Canadian supplier selling de-identified or synthetic task data, RL environments, or expert-authored content faces essentially the same friction as a US supplier. A Canadian supplier selling anything containing personal information of Canadians faces a genuine conflict when the buyer is a US lab [I, no source draws this distinction explicitly but it follows from the fact that the obligations attach to personal information].

The second is the **thin domestic buyer base** covered above. The third, **US procurement preference against Canadian suppliers, could not be evidenced in either direction and should not be asserted** [U]. The only procurement-preference evidence found relates to government contracting, which is a different market. Whether any lab has a stated data-residency or domicile requirement for suppliers was also not found [U].

One inference cuts against conventional Canadian positioning and deserves to be stated plainly: **Canadian data residency is more plausibly a sell-side liability than an asset in this specific market.** US labs train on US infrastructure, so a residency constraint is a cost to them rather than a feature, and the **2026 US Report on Trade Barriers targets data sovereignty measures worldwide** [V, [Michael Geist](https://www.michaelgeist.ca/), April 2026]. No source states this conclusion; flag it as inference [I].

## PIPEDA section 5(3) killed the scraping defence in May 2026

The regulatory layer changed materially five months before this research was conducted, and most commentary has not caught up.

### The decision that reframes everything

**PIPEDA Findings #2026-002**, the joint investigation of OpenAI by the federal Privacy Commissioner together with Quebec's CAI, BC's OIPC and Alberta's OIPC, issued **6 May 2026**, is the first Canadian regulatory decision squarely on the legality of acquiring data to train a commercial AI model [V, [priv.gc.ca](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2026/pipeda-2026-002/), 6 May 2026].

The binding constraint it identified is **not consent mechanics but s. 5(3), the "appropriate purposes" test, which operates independently of consent**. Regulators found that the "scope and scale of collecting from public websites/licensed sources" was "overbroad and therefore not necessary and proportional," that privacy harms were disproportionate to benefits, and that less privacy-invasive alternatives existed. The same conduct simultaneously contravened PIPA-BC ss. 2, 11, 14 and 17, PIPA-AB ss. 11, 16 and 19, and s. 5 of Quebec's Private Sector Act [V, same]. **Because s. 5(3) is not curable by consent, a broker cannot fix an over-broad corpus by retroactively obtaining permissions; the proportionality of the collection is assessed at the time it happened** [I].

Four consequences follow directly and each one matters to a data business.

**Training is a legitimate purpose; the manner of acquisition was not.** The regulators accepted developing ChatGPT as legitimate, and found collection from users' own interactions with the service proportionate and not well-founded as a complaint. The adverse finding attached to the bulk-scraped and licensed corpora [V, ¶104-106]. **The one clearly defensible acquisition channel in the decision is first-party data collected from a service's own users for a disclosed purpose** [I].

**Licensed data does not launder the problem.** Licensed datasets were under 1% of OpenAI's training data and were still swept into the adverse finding, flagged as potentially containing sensitive material from news sources describing criminal history or health status [V, ¶134]. **Buying data does not transfer away accountability**: OpenAI was found deficient for insufficient privacy impact assessment at the collection stage, limited documentation of mitigations, and no systematic approach to identifying sensitive information [V, Issue 7].

**The "publicly available" exception does not cover scraped web content, and this is textual rather than interpretive.** The *Regulations Specifying Publicly Available Information* (SOR/2001-7, registered 13 December 2000, **current to 21 September 2026**) list exactly five closed categories: public telephone directory listings where the subscriber can opt out; professional or business directory entries used for the directory's own purpose; statutory registries with a legal right of public access, used for the registry's purpose; records of judicial or quasi-judicial bodies, used for that purpose; and personal information appearing in a publication "**where the individual has provided the information**" [V, [laws-lois.justice.gc.ca, SOR/2001-7](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2001-7/page-1.html)]. The regulators confirmed these "specifically exclude social media and user-generated content platforms" [V, ¶160-162]. **SOR/2001-7 is the most consequential single instrument for a Canadian data broker, it has not been amended, and its five categories were drafted in 2000 for directories and court records. Any Canadian business model premised on "it's public, so we can collect it" is foreclosed by the regulation's text as read by four regulators** [I]. Even the publication category requires that the individual provided the information, so scraping a news archive does not clear third parties written *about* in the articles [I]. Alberta's version is narrower still: PIPA-AB "specifically requires 'it is reasonable to assume that the individual... provided that information'" [V, ¶162].

**A performance benchmark for PII filtering now exists.** The regulators examined OpenAI's post-investigation detection tool at the technical level and recorded **98-99% recall with a 3-6% false-positive rate**, evaluated against 80,000 synthetic chat excerpts, distinguishing private individuals from public figures, and accepted that it "can significantly reduce risk." By contrast, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4-era filtering removed "only a subset of personal identifiers" and "did not detect opinions, sensitive characteristics linked to identifiable persons," and did not exclude social media or children's sites [V, ¶88, ¶103, ¶108-114]. **A clearance business that cannot demonstrate comparable numbers has a documented regulatory yardstick working against it** [I]. And because the improved tool was not applied retroactively and OpenAI committed to **deprecate GPT-3.5 and GPT-4**, model deprecation is emerging as the de facto Canadian remedy for a tainted training corpus, which is an extreme cost and a strong argument for clearing data before training [I, ¶113-114].

Enforcement teeth are the current weak point federally. The matter was recorded as "well-founded and conditionally resolved" with no penalty [V, ¶115-116], **so today's federal enforcement risk is reputational and remedial rather than financial** [I; the statutory absence of AMP power under PIPEDA is widely understood but was not independently verified here, U]. OpenAI's jurisdictional objection was rejected, so foreign labs cannot escape Canadian jurisdiction by being foreign [V, ¶22-43].

### AIDA is dead and nothing replaced it

**Bill C-27, and with it AIDA, died on the order paper when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025** [V, [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/06/canada-tables-bill-c36-the-protecting-privacy-and-consumer-data-act), 17 June 2026]. **Canada has no comprehensive AI statute in force or before Parliament as of September 2026** [V, Chambers *Artificial Intelligence 2026, Canada*]. **For a data broker, the practical effect is that there is no AI-specific compliance layer to clear in Canada: the entire legal test for AI training data is privacy law plus copyright** [I].

What exists instead: **Bill C-36, the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act**, introduced at first reading on **15 June 2026** by Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation Evan Solomon, sitting at **second reading with no activity recorded** as of 29 September 2026, so PIPEDA remains operative [V, [LEGISinfo 45-1/C-36](https://www.parl.ca/legisinfo/en/bill/45-1/c-36); [McCarthy Tétrault](https://www.mccarthy.ca/en/insights/blogs/techlex/bill-c-36-what-organizations-need-to-know-about-canada-s-new-privacy-reform), 16 June 2026]. AI regulation was deliberately unbundled from privacy: "Bill C-36 abandons standalone AI legislation, addressing AI governance through privacy law, online safety legislation, and sector-specific measures instead" [V, DLA Piper]. C-36 would make express consent the default, expand consent exceptions to cover "business activities, research and de-identification," require documented privacy impact assessments before invoking exceptions, codify that "**de-identified personal information does not cease to be personal information**," prohibit re-identification with narrow exceptions, and require cross-border transfer risk assessments framed around data as a "strategic national asset." A new **Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada**, established by Bill C-34 (introduced 10 June 2026), would have **binding order authority**, with proposed AMPs up to the greater of **$10 million or 3% of gross annual global revenue** and criminal fines on indictment up to the greater of **$25 million or 5%** [V, DLA Piper 17 June 2026; [ISED news release](https://www.canada.ca/en/innovation-science-economic-development/news/2026/06/government-of-canada-tables-new-legislation-to-protect-childrens-data-strengthen-privacy-and-build-trust-in-the-digital-economy.html), 15 June 2026].

**C-36's "research and de-identification" consent exception, if enacted as described, would be the single most commercially significant change for a clearance business**, because it is the closest thing Canada would have to a lawful basis for processing personal data into training-ready datasets without consent. Its scope will be set largely by regulations that do not exist [I]. But given that the bill sat at second reading with no recorded activity for over three months, and that this is the third attempt in six years after C-11 and C-27 both died, **passage before 2027 is not assured** [I]. Whether C-36 contains any provision specifically addressing AI training data, data brokers, or the sale of personal information was checked against three separate sources and none identified one [U].

### Quebec is the binding jurisdiction

For a data business, Quebec matters more than PIPEDA, and by a wide margin. All section text below is from the consolidated English text at [LégisQuébec, CQLR c. P-39.1](https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/P-39.1), retrieved 29 September 2026 [V].

Consent under **s. 14** "must be clear, free and informed and be given for specific purposes... requested for each such purpose, in clear and simple language," presented separately from other information, valid "only for the time necessary," and consent not given in accordance with the Act "is without effect." **Section 12** blocks secondary use within the enterprise without consent, requires *express* consent for sensitive information, and permits five exceptions including use "necessary for study or research purposes or for the production of statistics **and if the information is de-identified**." The "consistent purpose" test is narrow and expressly excludes marketing: "commercial or philanthropic prospection may not be considered a consistent purpose." **Section 13** requires express consent for disclosure of sensitive information to third parties.

**Section 17 is the sharpest operational constraint on any Canada-to-US AI data pipeline.** Before communicating personal information outside Québec, an enterprise must conduct a privacy impact assessment weighing sensitivity, purposes, protection measures including contractual ones, and "the legal framework applicable in the State in which the information would be communicated." The transfer may proceed only if the assessment "establishes that it would receive adequate protection," and it "must be the subject of a written agreement." **Note that this applies to transfers outside Québec, including to the rest of Canada, not only outside the country.** It requires a documented finding of adequacy about US law, and US law's treatment of foreign personal data is precisely the thing being assessed; **unlike the EU, Quebec has published no adequacy list** [I; the absence of a list is U].

**Section 23 sets Canada's only statutory anonymization standard**: information is anonymized "if it is, **at all times, reasonably foreseeable in the circumstances that it irreversibly no longer allows the person to be identified directly or indirectly**," achieved "according to generally accepted best practices and according to the criteria and terms determined by regulation." The **Anonymization Regulation**, published in the *Gazette officielle du Québec* on **15 May 2024**, requires anonymization "carried out under the supervision of a person qualified in the field," assesses effectiveness against three criteria (**individualization, correlation and inference**), and requires a **register of anonymizations** [V, McCarthy Tétrault and Fasken bulletins, May 2024]. Quebec's *de-identified* standard is much weaker (s. 12: no longer allows the person to be *directly* identified), and the two terms are legally distinct and not interchangeable.

**Quebec treats anonymization as a process with an audit trail, not a data state.** A broker selling "anonymized" datasets into Quebec needs the qualified-person supervision, the three-criteria assessment and the register, or it cannot substantiate the claim [I]. And the cost of that process is high enough that it functions as a barrier to entry favouring larger, better-capitalized intermediaries [I].

The penalty structure is what makes Quebec bite. **AMPs (ss. 90.1, 90.12) reach $50,000 for a natural person and otherwise $10,000,000 or, if greater, 2% of worldwide turnover.** **Penal offences (s. 91) range from $5,000-$100,000 for a natural person and $15,000-$25,000,000, or if greater 4% of worldwide turnover, for others**, and s. 91(5) makes it an offence to identify or attempt to identify a natural person "using de-identified information without the authorization of the person holding the information **or using anonymized information**." **Directors who ordered, authorized or consented are parties to the offence (s. 93).** And **s. 93.1 guarantees punitive damages of not less than $1,000** where an intentional or grossly negligent infringement causes injury, **which makes class actions economically viable without proof of quantified loss** [I]. Quebec's CAI is an active AI regulator, not a theoretical one: it was a full co-investigator in the OpenAI matter [V, #2026-002].

**Is "data broker" a regulated category in Canada?** Essentially no. There is no Canadian equivalent to a US-state-style data broker registry. The one registration regime is **Quebec's "personal information agent"** under **s. 70**, and it is narrower than "data broker": it captures those who "on a commercial basis... establish files on other persons and prepare and communicate to third parties **credit reports bearing on the character, reputation or solvency**" of those persons. Registered agents face accuracy duties (s. 71), access and rectification procedures (s. 78), mandatory public website disclosure (s. 79), and a hard **seven-year destruction rule (s. 79.1)**, the only hard retention ceiling found anywhere in Canadian private-sector privacy law. Registration confers no endorsement: "A personal information agent may not invoke registration with the Commission to claim that the agent's competence, solvency, conduct or operations are recognized or approved" (s. 70.1) [V, all from LégisQuébec P-39.1]. **A firm acquiring and reselling data for AI training would generally not be a personal information agent, so there is no licence to operate as an AI-data broker anywhere in Canada, which means no registration barrier to entry but also no registration-based safe harbour. Market credibility has to be built on demonstrable provenance practice** [I]. Provincial consumer-reporting statutes (Ontario's *Consumer Reporting Act* and its equivalents) are the other place a data broker can be a registered category and **were not researched** [U].

### Alberta, BC, Nova Scotia and the residency patchwork

Both PIPA-BC and PIPA-AB apply the same reasonableness gate as PIPEDA s. 5(3), and both were applied concurrently against OpenAI. A federal exemption order exists for BC, but it did not oust OIPC-BC's concurrent jurisdiction in a joint investigation [V, #2026-002, ¶34-40, ¶59]. **The operative compliance reality for a national broker is not "which statute applies" but that all four apply at once to the same corpus, and the most restrictive provision governs** [I]. Detailed textual comparison of the three statutes was not completed [U].

On public-sector residency: **BC liberalized in 2021.** Bill 22 removed FOIPPA's general prohibition on disclosure, storage or access outside Canada; s. 33.1 now permits disclosure outside Canada "in accordance with the regulations," with an assessment required where *sensitive* personal information is stored outside Canada [V, [BC FOIPPA Manual s. 33.1](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/governments/services-for-government/policies-procedures/foippa-manual/disclosure-outside-canada)]. **Nova Scotia did not.** PIIDPA s. 5(1) requires that personal information in a public body's custody "is stored only in Canada and accessed only in Canada," subject to consent, permitted disclosures, or a head-of-public-body allowance "to meet the necessary requirements of the public body's operation," and s. 2(3) makes the Act paramount over conflicting enactments [V, [NS PIIDPA](https://nslegislature.ca/sites/default/files/legc/statutes/persinfo.htm)]. The practical consequence is a **bifurcated market: private-sector Canadian data can flow to US labs with assessments and contracts, but public-sector-sourced data (health, education, municipal, provincial administrative) is largely closed to a US-processing model in Nova Scotia and requires sensitive-data assessments in BC** [I].

### Copyright: no exception, no precedent, and two live cases

**Canada has no text and data mining exception in the Copyright Act.** ISED's consultation ran 12 October 2023 to 15 January 2024 and produced a *What We Heard* report on **11 February 2025** that confirmed "Canada's Copyright Act does not currently contain a specific text and data mining exception" and that "Canadian courts have not yet commented specifically on these questions." Submissions split sharply: cultural creators and collective societies opposed any new exception and favoured licensing, including a preference for "voluntary or opt-in licensing" over opt-out, while the technology sector wanted a clarification that TDM "does not infringe copyright." The government committed only to "consider options." **No legislation followed** [V, [ISED What We Heard report](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/strategic-policy-sector/en/marketplace-framework-policy/consultation-copyright-age-generative-artificial-intelligence-what-we-heard-report), 11 Feb 2025].

Meanwhile the question is being litigated in two places. A coalition of **CBC/Radio-Canada, Postmedia, Metroland, Toronto Star, The Globe and Mail and The Canadian Press sued OpenAI in the Ontario Superior Court on 29 November 2024**, seeking punitive damages and disgorgement [V, [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/business/openai-canadian-lawsuit-1.7396940)]. On **7 November 2025** the court ruled the claims can proceed in Ontario, reasoning that "the fair and efficient working of the legal system in Canada favours allowing Canadian authors of Canadian-created Works with claims against foreign companies for breaches of Canadian copyright and other laws to pursue those claims here." **The ruling does not address the merits** [V, [Carters](https://www.carters.ca/jurisdictional-ruling-opens-door-for-canadian-copyright-claims-against-ai-companies/); [CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/openai-new-publishers-lawsuit-ontario-9.6995520)]. Combined with the Cohere case in SDNY, **the configuration is the worst possible one for a data broker: no safe harbour to rely on, jurisdiction confirmed over foreign labs, and no precedent establishing that training is fair dealing. The entire copyright layer of a clearance business must be built on contract, not statute** [I]. Because ISED's report records stakeholder support for training-data recordkeeping obligations, **provenance documentation is the capability most likely to become a statutory requirement, and is the one a clearance business should build for** [I].

### Health privacy: permissive statute, prohibitive incentives

PHIPA and its provincial equivalents **do not prohibit commercial use of properly de-identified health data.** Custodians may de-identify PHI without patient consent, provided they comply with PHIPA's general requirements, and recommended practice is to describe routine de-identification in a public privacy statement and impose contractual obligations on recipients [V, Fasken analysis of PHIPA via search snippet; **the ontario.ca e-Laws fetch failed, so the statutory quotes are second-hand**]. PHIPA also prohibits re-identification with limited exceptions.

**The binding constraint is the reasonable-foreseeability standard, not a prohibition.** PHIPA's definition of de-identify turns on whether it is "reasonably foreseeable in the circumstances that it could be utilized, either alone or with other information, to identify the individual." That is a moving target that gets harder to satisfy as external datasets proliferate, so a custodian cannot certify compliance in advance against an unknown future linkage attack, and counsel advises against release [I]. **Liability sits entirely with the custodian and benefit sits with the licensee**: the hospital must de-identify correctly, publish practices and impose contractual obligations, carrying the regulatory and reputational downside, while the AI company carries the upside. **This asymmetry, not the statute, is why "not sharing data can become a default"** [I, from the structure plus Boyer's quote].

The proof that this is institutional rather than statutory is that the enabling paperwork was invented this year. **INOVAIT**, the federally funded image-guided therapy and AI network hosted by Sunnybrook Research Institute, published its **Data Licensing Toolkit Version 1.0 on or about 5 January 2026**, to "support Canadian healthcare institutions in establishing governance processes for responsible and trustworthy sharing of de-identified health data with industry." Its core instrument is a **Data Request Evaluation Scorecard** validated and used by the Data Access Committee at Sunnybrook, and it is **free to hospitals and not-for-profits under a non-exclusive licensing agreement**. The explicit blocker it names: "the health data these companies need most often resides with Canadian healthcare institutions that are reluctant to share data with commercial partners due to caution stemming from a **lack of clarity in privacy legislation**" [V, [INOVAIT](https://inovait.ca/data) and Businesswire release 20260105935834, 5 Jan 2026; the Businesswire page returned 403 so this rests on the snippet plus the fetched INOVAIT page]. **A market whose standard contract templates are a nine-month-old free public good is a pre-market** [I]. INOVAIT's own framework further **limits licensing to "public-good opportunities"** and is built on transparency, patient and public representation, defined governance, and Indigenous data sovereignty rights [V, inovait.ca/data], which signals that **the emerging Canadian norm is access for collaborative development, not sale of a corpus** [I].

Quebec is the most restrictive jurisdiction for commercial secondary use of health data given Law 25's express-consent requirement for sensitive data, with de-identified research as the narrow carve-out [I, verify against the statute]. And the **ten-plus distinct provincial regimes make a pan-Canadian health licence structurally expensive**: a buyer wanting national-scale data must satisfy PHIPA, Alberta's HIA, Quebec Law 25, BC's FIPPA and PIPA and the rest separately, with different consent standards. **That transaction cost alone would deter most buyers even if every custodian were willing** [I].

Canada's own diagnosis says the same thing. The **Pan-Canadian Health Data Strategy** Expert Advisory Group Report 3, chaired by Dr. Vivek Goel and published **3 May 2022** [**STALE at 4 years, but still the canonical diagnosis**], conceded there is "no single organization mandated to provide stewardship" and "no coherent leadership across jurisdictions," recommended a Health Information Stewardship Council and Chief Health Data Stewards, and estimated the required federal investment at **approximately $1 billion annually sustained over 10+ years**. Its conclusion: "The fundamental challenge we face is not one of technology, rather it is one of culture," requiring the overcoming of a "legacy of mistrust between jurisdictions" [V, canada.ca PCHDS Report 3]. **The recommended Stewardship Council does not appear to have been created** [I, from September 2026 commentary still describing governance as an open question]. The EU took the opposite path: the **European Health Data Space took effect in March 2025**, covering 17 data categories across 27 member states with enumerated secondary uses, where **Canada published principles** [V, [Policy Options](https://policyoptions.irpp.org/2026/09/canada-health-data-space), Sept 2026]. **A licensee needs an enumerated permission, not a principle, and that is the precise legal gap** [I].

Two exceptions to all of this are worth flagging. **Genomics is the most brokerable Canadian health data category** because consent is obtained prospectively and explicitly contemplates commercial use: CMAJ guidance states consent documents "should make clear how the findings will be used for future research or that the data may be used commercially for the development of new diagnostic tests, new drugs or other commercial products, for which participants will not receive any share of profits or compensation" [V, [CMAJ](https://www.cmaj.ca/) 194(44):E1500, 2022, **4 years old**]. Add Genome Canada's **$96M Genome Applications Partnership Program** commercialization mandate [V, genomecanada.ca via search snippet, date not established] and GA4GH-standard interoperability, and genomics has three properties administrative health data lacks entirely [I]. The consent norm that participants receive no share of profits also removes a benefit-sharing negotiation that would otherwise block licensing [I]. The second exception is the AI scribe channel described earlier.

Finally, a demand-side regulatory driver worth noting: **Health Canada published Pre-market Guidance for Machine Learning-Enabled Medical Devices on 1 April 2026**, introducing the Predetermined Change Control Plan and setting requirements for data quality, training, validation, bias management, performance monitoring and transparency [V, search snippet citing canada.ca, April 2026]. Canada's Drug Agency has separately issued guidance on reporting AI methods used to extract information from unstructured health data to generate real-world evidence, explicitly covering LLMs [V, [CDA-AMC](https://www.cda-amc.ca/news/new-guidance-issued-reporting-ai-methods-used-generate-real-world-evidence), date not established]. **A national regulator writing rules for LLM-derived real-world evidence creates compliance-driven demand for traceable, documented, well-governed training data, which raises the relative value of provenanced Canadian datasets against scraped ones** [I].

### Indigenous data sovereignty is the least negotiable constraint

**OCAP, Ownership, Control, Access and Possession, is not statute.** It is a First Nations ethics code originating in the First Nations Regional Longitudinal Health Survey, administered by the First Nations Information Governance Centre, and it is a registered trademark. Its force comes through research ethics policy, institutional review, research agreements and community consent [V, [TCPS 2 (2022) Chapter 9](https://ethics.gc.ca/eng/tcps2-eptc2_2022_chapter9-chapitre9.html); [FNIGC via University of Saskatchewan](https://libguides.usask.ca/RDM/IndigenousDataSovereignty)]. **Important sourcing caveat: fnigc.ca returned HTTP 403 to every automated access attempt across two separate research threads, so all OCAP detail in this report is second-hand and FNIGC's primary materials should be read directly before acting on any of it** [flagged limitation].

Canada's tri-council research ethics policy states that OCAP "addresses issues of privacy, intellectual property, data custody and secondary use of data," and that where divergences exist between institutional policy and community codes "they should be addressed and resolved **prior to the commencement of the research**." Commercialization is expressly flagged as a harm: "Appropriation of collective knowledge, treatment of such knowledge as a commodity to be traded, or making unauthorized adaptations for commercial purposes, may cause offence or harm to communities from which the knowledge originates." Secondary use requires its own community engagement and consent. And critically for any de-identification workflow: "Small Indigenous communities are characterized by dense networks of relationships. As a result, **coding individual data is often not sufficient to mask identities, even when data are aggregated**" [V, all TCPS 2 (2022) Ch. 9]. Inuit practice uses possession agreements distinct from research agreements, set out in an MOU with the land claim organization, continuing "long after the research is completed" [V, same].

Institutionally this is advancing, not receding. FNIGC has advanced the **First Nations Data Governance Strategy** focused on "the repatriation of First Nations data sets from federal agencies"; federal agencies continue implementing the **UNDRIP Act (2021)** and an Indigenous Data Strategy; the **2023-2026 Data Strategy for the Federal Public Service** commits to "a whole-of-government approach to the management and sharing of Indigenous data"; and the Native Women's Association of Canada published an **Indigenous Data Sovereignty Toolkit in March 2026** [V, [IWGIA](https://iwgia.org/en/indigenous-data-sovereignty/6018-iw-2026-indigenous-data-sovereignty.html); [CSPS](https://www.csps-efpc.gc.ca/tools/articles/indigenous-data-sovereignty-eng.aspx); [NWAC](https://nwac-afac.ca/assets-documents/Indigenous-Data-Sovereignty-Toolkit-EN.pdf)].

Three inferences follow. **OCAP's practical force in a commercial transaction runs through institutional research ethics approval, the contractual terms of data-sharing and possession agreements that survive the project, and consultation obligations. None of them is privacy legislation, and all three can block a transaction** [I]. **A de-identification workflow calibrated for a general population will not satisfy either the ethics standard or, plausibly, Quebec's inference criterion, for First Nations community data** [I]. And **individual consent is insufficient where the data is collective; community-level authorization is contemplated in addition** [I].

Two specific exposures are worth naming. **Much Canadian resource data is *about* land subject to treaty, title or consultation obligations, so any drill-core, lidar or site-telemetry dataset originating on or near such land carries a governance claim that OCAP is designed to assert. A broker acquiring a distressed operator's dataset would inherit the claim without inheriting the relationships needed to resolve it** [I]. And **the CDCL's silence on Indigenous data governance across every release reviewed is notable**: a $55M+ federal geoscience digitization program spanning ten jurisdictions with no stated Indigenous data-governance framework in its public communications is an unresolved exposure [I, from a negative reading across the NRCan, Newswire, Nunavut News and CDL sources; a framework may exist and simply not appear in press materials]. Separately, **no source was found connecting OCAP specifically to industrial or operational data licensing** as opposed to health, research and statistical data, where the literature is concentrated. That is a genuine gap, not a finding of no applicability [U].

Finally, the barrier nobody talks about but the CDCL priced exactly: **provincial jurisdiction over natural resources fragments every resource dataset across 13 subnational regimes.** The federal government needed **10 separate provincial and territorial participation agreements plus a $2.16M side investment just to bring Saskatchewan's collection in**, and is offering provinces **up to $5M apiece** to digitize their own holdings [V, Newswire and Nunavut News, Sept 2026]. **Even the federal government must pay provinces to release resource data it does not control, which is the structural reason no single-buyer national Canadian resource dataset can be assembled privately** [I].

## A US$6 billion vendor market with no Canadian participant

### The sizing is broken, so use a different method

Syndicated analyst estimates for the 2026 AI training-dataset market range from **US$3.4B to US$11.91B**, a 3.5x spread on the same nominal category in the same year: Straits Research at US$3.4B, Research and Markets at US$3.87B, Grand View at US$3.9B, Fortune Business Insights at US$4.44B, Business Research Insights at US$7.48B, and Mordor Intelligence at US$11.91B [V, each per its own published report]. Mordor's own two reports differ by roughly 5x depending on whether the label is "training dataset" or "data labeling." Growth estimates cluster around **21.6% to 28.9% CAGR** [V, [Technavio](https://www.technavio.com/report/ai-training-dataset-market-industry-analysis) and others].

**None of these should be quoted as authoritative, and the more defensible conclusion is that they are measuring the wrong category.** Adding only the reported top-line of the five largest vendors gives roughly **US$6B+ in 2026 vendor revenue before counting Turing, Invisible, TELUS Digital, Appen, iMerit, Centific, Prime Intellect and forty-plus smaller vendors**, which already exceeds four of the six analyst estimates [I]. The low estimates are consistent with a legacy image and text annotation services business; the high ones are consistent with including RLHF, expert domain data and RL environments [I]. **For an investor-grade picture, use the bottom-up vendor-revenue method and present the analyst range as evidence of measurement failure rather than as a sizing** [I]. A separate bottom-up industry estimate puts annual industry revenue at ~US$8.5B with a "static labeling" core of ~US$5B growing 50%+ annually [V as reported, [Pebblous](https://blog.pebblous.ai/blog/ai-data-supply-oligopoly/en/), 2026; **vendor blog, directional only**].

One methodological warning that matters more than any single number: **headline "ARR" in this category is systematically inflated by roughly 2.5 to 3x versus net revenue for the marketplace models**, because contractors receive 60-70% of top line. Scale AI Inc. and Surge run services P&Ls rather than marketplace take rates, so **a comparison table that puts Mercor's $2B next to Scale's $1B is comparing gross to net** [I, from the pass-through documented at both Mercor and micro1].

That cannot be explained by the Google contract [I]. **The surviving Appen business is a low-cost-geography business, which is the clearest available signal that commodity annotation is a cost-of-labour game that high-wage geographies lose** [I].

### Where the capital is going, and where it is not

Capital is flowing hard into expert-marketplace and RL-environment supply and not at all into commodity annotation tooling. The verified disclosed rounds from roughly February 2025 to September 2026 total **well over US$1.1B into human-data and environment vendors** [V, per-round sources above; this is a floor, not a total, since Crunchbase and PitchBook aggregate views are paywalled]. The bifurcation is the story: **the top ten data-labeling *software* companies have raised US$271M between them, ever**, while a single expert-marketplace round (Mercor's Series C) was US$350M [V, [Seedtable](https://seedtable.com/best-data-labeling-software-startups)].

Valuations are running at extreme multiples of *net* revenue. Mercor at US$10B on roughly US$400-600M annualized net is 17-25x; at the rumoured US$20B it is 33-50x. **These are software multiples applied to a business with a 30-40% take rate and human variable cost** [I]. And **the Meta-Scale deal appears to have accelerated funding into challengers rather than chilling it**: Mercor's US$10B round (Oct 2025), Invisible's round (Sept 2025) and Surge's first-ever raise (July 2025) all cluster in the four months after the June 2025 deal [I, from the date sequence].

On margins, the honest picture has two completely different structures wearing the same label. **Reusable, off-the-shelf and synthetic data reaches 80-90% gross margin** because a single dataset is sold to multiple customers [V, [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/20/ai-data-startup-micro1-reaches-500m-gross-run-rate-amid-ai-training-boom/), 20 Aug 2026, the highest-quality margin source found]. **Marketplace take rate is 30-40% before opex** [V, Sacra on Mercor; TechCrunch on micro1]. Scale AI Inc. is reported to monetize per task at 50%+ gross margin [V as reported, HeroHunt, **low source quality**]. Meanwhile **commodity labeling wages have roughly halved since 2022** and **foundation models now correctly pre-label 60-90% of common cases**, leaving humans the hard edges [V as reported, HeroHunt, 2026, **low source quality but directionally consistent with Appen's filings**]. The premium segments that still hold price are **medical imaging, 3D LiDAR and legal document annotation** [V as reported, same source]. **No audited gross margin exists for any of Scale, Surge, Mercor, Handshake, Turing, Invisible or micro1** [U].

### The commoditization argument, honestly assessed

The evidence is genuinely mixed and the honest answer is that **the bottom of the market is commoditizing fast and the top is not, yet.**

For commoditization: models pre-label most common cases; wages have halved; **the vendors themselves are automating humans out** (micro1 is increasingly generating synthetic data without human involvement and selling single datasets to multiple customers at 80-90% margin); labs demonstrably can and do terminate; every major lab is building environment teams in-house while buying; and before Scale AI Inc. existed, labelling was done in-house, so the outsourced model is itself recent and reversible [V, per sources above].

Against: lab spend is rising; **Mercor grew gross revenue 70% YoY into H1 2026 and Handshake's AI line grew 349% YoY to April 2026, and if labs were internalising, the challengers' growth would be decelerating rather than accelerating** [I]; internal teams reportedly "struggle to match the tooling breadth and compliance certifications achieved by specialized vendors"; and enterprises are "unlikely to share proprietary workflow data directly with AI labs," which is the structural reason an intermediary exists at all [V, [Sacra on Mercor](https://sacra.com/c/mercor/)].

**The strongest single correction to the conventional narrative: the "labs said they would source data themselves" claim is not supported as a general trend.** The three documented pullbacks (Google, OpenAI, Microsoft) were all from *one* vendor, Scale AI Inc., all within weeks of *one* event, Meta's 49% stake, and the spend moved to Surge, Mercor and Handshake, which is why those three grew triple-digit in the same window. **That was vendor substitution driven by a conflict of interest, not disintermediation** [I]. **The real commoditization risk is to the task type, not to the category**: routine annotation is being automated, while expert judgment is what buyers pay escalating prices for [I].

### Acquisitions, and one correction that must not propagate

There have been exactly two structurally important transactions, and only one was an acquisition.

**Meta's US$14.3B for 49% of Scale AI Inc. (June 2025)** reshaped the market not because of the price but because it made a shared supplier untrustworthy and split the category [I]. **Before June 2025 a single vendor could serve every lab; after it, ownership by any lab or hyperscaler is disqualifying.** That is the structural opening Surge, Mercor, Handshake and micro1 all walked through in the following twelve months [I]. **Strategic neutrality is now a priced asset**: Surge explicitly markets having no Big Tech ownership and micro1 markets that it does not sell to Chinese model makers. **Provenance and alignment have become product features, not compliance overhead** [I].

The genuine M&A that followed is small and strategic. **EXL (NASDAQ: EXLS) completed its acquisition of iMerit on 3 August 2026, described as "a recognized leader in AI model training, evaluation and reinforcement learning," for up to US$310M: US$170M upfront plus US$140M in earnouts and incentives** [V, [EXL newsroom](https://www.exlservice.com/about/newsroom/exl-completes-acquisition-of-imerit-accelerating-enterprise-ai-leadership); [StockTitan](https://www.stocktitan.net/news/EXLS/exl-to-acquire-i-merit-advancing-its-leadership-in-enterprise-ai-by-qbi1k7ukks05.html)]. **Over half the consideration is contingent, so the buyer did not underwrite the revenue** [I].

**CORRECTION TO CARRY FORWARD: a widely-surfaced claim that "EXL acquired Sama in August 2026" is FALSE. The target was iMerit, not Sama.** This was checked directly against EXL's own newsroom [V]. The claim appears in search results and must not be allowed to propagate.

Two inferences on exit shape matter for anyone scoping a build. **The buyer set for a training-data company is services roll-ups, not labs**, because labs now have strong reason not to own a data vendor: owning one poisons it for everyone else, which is exactly what happened to Scale AI Inc. [I]. **A data brokerage's realistic exit is a strategic services buyer at a services multiple, not a lab at a software multiple** [I]. And **the iMerit price is a sobering comparable**: a recognized category leader cleared at US$170M cash, against Mercor at US$10B, a 60x gap between the marketplace narrative and the services reality [I].

## Where the gaps are real, and the steelman for walking away

Everything above is reporting. This section is analysis, and it is separated deliberately.

### The gaps that are genuine

**Winter and adverse-weather driving data is a genuinely scarce global asset, and Canada is one of very few jurisdictions that can generate it at scale in dense urban conditions.** The Canadian Adverse Driving Conditions dataset is described by its authors as the first autonomous driving dataset focused specifically on adverse conditions: **7,000 annotated frames from 8 cameras, a VLP-32C lidar and a Novatel GNSS+INS, across 75 driving sequences covering over 20 km, collected over 3 days in March 2018 and February 2019**, produced by Waterloo's WISE Lab and Toronto's TRAIL Lab [V, [arXiv:2001.10117](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10117); [IJRR 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0278364920979368)]. Toronto's **Boreas** dataset offers **over 350 km across a full year of seasonal variation including falling snow**, making it Canada's most substantial winter AV asset on distance [V, [arXiv:2203.10168](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.10168)]. The competing datasets are both small, US and non-commercially licensed: WADS (Michigan, 20 scenes) and Ithaca365 (Cornell, 15 km route), both CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 [V, [SPIE](https://www.spiedigitallibrary.org/journals/optical-engineering/volume-62/issue-3/031207/Winter-adverse-driving-dataset-for-autonomy-in-inclement-winter-weather/10.1117/1.OE.62.3.031207.full); [Ithaca365](https://ithaca365.mae.cornell.edu/)]. **CADC is the single most commercially accessible Canadian AV dataset because it offers a commercial-licence pathway rather than a flat non-commercial bar, and nobody is documented as ever having taken that licence** [V that the arrangement is stated, via [Segments.ai](https://segments.ai/blog/lidar-driving-datasets/); **the official host cadcd.uwaterloo.ca refused an HTTPS connection during research, which is itself relevant to whether the pathway is live**].

But **the edge is entirely latent, and it is being captured by someone else.** At 7,000 frames and 20 km, CADC's commercial value is as a benchmark and validation set, not a training corpus [I]. The largest collector of labelled, sensor-complete, adverse-weather Canadian edge cases through 2026 will be **Gatik, a US company running 50 trucks through GTA winters with no obligation to share anything beyond confidential regulatory reporting** [I]. Meanwhile Ontario's ACMV pilot, which runs **1 August 2025 to 1 August 2035** and mandates daily recording of video, sensor data, weather, GPS timestamps at intervals not exceeding one minute, driver interventions and automated system status, with three-year retention, monthly trip records and annual reporting of **disengagements**, hard braking and safety-critical events, will accumulate one of the richest government-held longitudinal records of commercial AV operation anywhere [V, [ontario.ca ACMV pilot conditions](https://www.ontario.ca/page/automated-commercial-motor-vehicle-pilot-program-conditions)]. And it is structurally unavailable: **"MTO will treat all data from individual carriers as confidential," with only aggregate and non-commercially-sensitive data shareable with municipalities or Transport Canada** [V, same]. **The carrier owns the data, the regulator holds a confidential copy it cannot commercialise, and there is therefore no supply side for a Canadian AV data market, which explains the absence of a demand side better than any claim about buyer interest** [I].

**Sanctuary AI's tactile-plus-teleoperation corpus is the most globally differentiated robot-learning data asset in Canada**, because visual-tactile paired demonstration data is rarer than vision-only demonstration data and Sanctuary's hand hardware is unusually instrumented [I]. Set that against the fact that **XDOF raised US$70M to build robot-data pipelines, and AI labs are already paying it to collect robot training data** [V, [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/17/collecting-robot-training-data-is-dirty-unglamorous-work-some-ai-labs-are-already-paying-xdof-to-do-it/), 17 June 2026], and **there is a real, identifiable arbitrage sitting unexploited: a paying global market and a Canadian producer of a scarcer variant of the same data, with no observed transaction between them** [I]. No source establishes that Sanctuary has ever sold or licensed data [U].

**The distressed-acquisition channel is proven but unexploited.** Canada's CCAA process is a functioning, court-supervised mechanism for acquiring data-bearing assets cheaply, and UrtheCast proves it works. But the pricing precedent cuts both ways: Deimos sold for US$3.2M and Farmers Edge's public equity for cents, which implies the market currently values these datasets at near zero when detached from a going-concern customer relationship. **A brokerage buying at those prices is buying cheap; a brokerage trying to resell at meaningful prices faces the same market that produced those valuations** [I]. **Farmers Edge is the specific asset most worth diligencing: a multi-country, multi-million-acre agronomic sensor dataset now sitting inside an insurance holding company with no obvious AI-training monetization motive** [I]. What Fairfax has done with it since 2024 is a live, checkable question with no evidence found [U]. **No Canadian distressed-asset buyer, fund or advisory was identified as specifically targeting data assets** [U].

**Earth observation is where a Canadian data brokerage would find an actual functioning market with pricing precedent**, because SkyWatch already proves API-mediated third-party EO resale works commercially in Canada [I]. And **subsurface and remote-sensing data is the realistic Canadian entry point for any industrial data business**, because provenance, format standards (PPDM) and an existing willingness to pay all already exist there, whereas for factory or mine-site telemetry none of the three does [I]. Note though that **whether any Canadian EO provider has licensed imagery specifically for foundation-model or geospatial-model training: no evidence found** [U].

**Genomics is the most brokerable Canadian health data category**, for the three reasons set out in the regulatory section, and it should be contrasted sharply with administrative health data, which has none of those properties [I].

And **Waabi's simulation-first architecture paired with a scarce real-world winter corpus is, on paper, the strongest strategic pairing in Canadian AV, because simulation is most valuable when seeded with rare real data and winter is the rarest. ### What would have to be true for the counter-case to fail

These are the falsifiable conditions, stated so they can be tested rather than argued [I throughout]. The venture sells **expert judgment in a regulated vertical**, not annotation, so global wage arbitrage does not apply. It owns an **exclusive or high-switching-cost supply relationship**, such as a professional body, a licensing regime or an institutional partnership, rather than a contractor pool anyone can poach. It sells **provenance and jurisdictional attestation as the product**, which is the one thing the Meta-Scale episode proved buyers will switch over. Its first customers are **enterprises with data they will not give a lab directly**, not the six frontier labs. And it is capitalised to survive a customer loss of 30%+ of revenue with no notice.

Read against the Canadian evidence, that profile points to one shape more than any other: **the intersection of categories F and G for regulated sector data, meaning rights-cleared, consent-documented, provenance-auditable Canadian-origin health or industrial data.** The supporting conditions are real: ICES-scale health assets, 15+ years of ICD-10, a national regulator writing LLM-derived-RWE guidance, the C.D. Howe diagnosis that Canada "has strong AI research and rich longitudinal health data, but lacks the commercialization architecture" [V, [C.D. Howe Institute](https://cdhowe.org/publication/scaling-up-commercializing-health-ai-in-canada/), publication date not established, **verify before quoting**], and Private AI and Replica Analytics proving the de-identification and synthesis layer is buildable here. **This is an inference, not a finding, and the steelman above remains unrebutted on the demand side** [I].

## Conclusion

The most useful way to hold this landscape is not as a market with gaps but as **an hourglass with an empty middle**. Canada is thick at the bottom, where the data is produced: methane plumes over the oil sands, drill core from ten jurisdictions, 13.4 million Ontarians' longitudinal health records, minute-resolution telemetry from autonomous trucks in Toronto winters, tactile demonstrations from humanoid hands in Vancouver. It is thick at the top, where models are built and where one Vancouver company processes two billion labels a year. **And it is almost empty in the layer that turns raw sector data into lab-ready training assets, which is precisely the layer that captures the most spend per employee anywhere in the world.** That emptiness is not an oversight waiting to be corrected. It is produced by four forces acting together: no domestic frontier-lab buyer to embed with, a wage structure that loses the commodity fight before it starts, provincial jurisdiction that fragments every national dataset into thirteen negotiations, and a liability structure in which the custodian carries all the risk while the licensee captures all the upside.

Two things follow that were not obvious going in. The first is that **the single most important regulatory development in this space is not an AI law at all, it is a thirty-year-old privacy statute and a regulation drafted in 2000.** AIDA is dead, no AI act is coming, and the entire legal test for Canadian training data is PIPEDA section 5(3) plus copyright contract. Four regulators used that section in May 2026 to find a frontier lab's collection unlawful independent of consent, and SOR/2001-7's five closed categories foreclose the "it's public" defence by their text. Any Canadian data business is therefore competing on provenance discipline, not on legal innovation, and the good news buried in that is that **provenance discipline is exactly the thing the Meta-Scale episode proved buyers will switch vendors over, and the thing nobody in the top four actually sells as an auditable product.** The second is that **Canadian data has been leaving the country steadily and profitably for six years, and not once through a data licence.** It left through Ocado's US$262M, Rockwell's US$550M, Aetion's acquisition of Replica, ALS's purchase of GoldSpot, Enverus's US$100M for a well-log library with no Canadian party at the table, Lafayette's bankruptcy bid for Attabotics, and Gatik's trucks quietly filling US servers with Ontario winter. **The equity channel works, the licensing channel does not exist, and anyone building here should understand that they would be inventing the second channel rather than competing in it.** That is a much harder thing than entering a market, and it is also the only honest description of what the opportunity actually is.
