The regulatory layer changed materially five months before this research was conducted, and most commentary has not caught up.

The decision that reframes everything

PIPEDA Findings #2026-002, the joint investigation of OpenAI by the federal Privacy Commissioner together with Quebec's CAI, BC's OIPC and Alberta's OIPC, issued 6 May 2026, is the first Canadian regulatory decision squarely on the legality of acquiring data to train a commercial AI model [V, priv.gc.ca, 6 May 2026].

The binding constraint it identified is not consent mechanics but s. 5(3), the "appropriate purposes" test, which operates independently of consent. Regulators found that the "scope and scale of collecting from public websites/licensed sources" was "overbroad and therefore not necessary and proportional," that privacy harms were disproportionate to benefits, and that less privacy-invasive alternatives existed. The same conduct simultaneously contravened PIPA-BC ss. 2, 11, 14 and 17, PIPA-AB ss. 11, 16 and 19, and s. 5 of Quebec's Private Sector Act [V, same]. Because s. 5(3) is not curable by consent, a broker cannot fix an over-broad corpus by retroactively obtaining permissions; the proportionality of the collection is assessed at the time it happened [I].

Four consequences follow directly and each one matters to a data business.

Training is a legitimate purpose; the manner of acquisition was not. The regulators accepted developing ChatGPT as legitimate, and found collection from users' own interactions with the service proportionate and not well-founded as a complaint. The adverse finding attached to the bulk-scraped and licensed corpora [V, ¶104-106]. The one clearly defensible acquisition channel in the decision is first-party data collected from a service's own users for a disclosed purpose [I].

Licensed data does not launder the problem. Licensed datasets were under 1% of OpenAI's training data and were still swept into the adverse finding, flagged as potentially containing sensitive material from news sources describing criminal history or health status [V, ¶134]. Buying data does not transfer away accountability: OpenAI was found deficient for insufficient privacy impact assessment at the collection stage, limited documentation of mitigations, and no systematic approach to identifying sensitive information [V, Issue 7].

The "publicly available" exception does not cover scraped web content, and this is textual rather than interpretive. The *Regulations Specifying Publicly Available Information* (SOR/2001-7, registered 13 December 2000, current to 21 September 2026) list exactly five closed categories: public telephone directory listings where the subscriber can opt out; professional or business directory entries used for the directory's own purpose; statutory registries with a legal right of public access, used for the registry's purpose; records of judicial or quasi-judicial bodies, used for that purpose; and personal information appearing in a publication "where the individual has provided the information" [V, laws-lois.justice.gc.ca, SOR/2001-7]. The regulators confirmed these "specifically exclude social media and user-generated content platforms" [V, ¶160-162]. SOR/2001-7 is the most consequential single instrument for a Canadian data broker, it has not been amended, and its five categories were drafted in 2000 for directories and court records. Any Canadian business model premised on "it's public, so we can collect it" is foreclosed by the regulation's text as read by four regulators [I]. Even the publication category requires that the individual provided the information, so scraping a news archive does not clear third parties written *about* in the articles [I]. Alberta's version is narrower still: PIPA-AB "specifically requires 'it is reasonable to assume that the individual... provided that information'" [V, ¶162].

A performance benchmark for PII filtering now exists. The regulators examined OpenAI's post-investigation detection tool at the technical level and recorded 98-99% recall with a 3-6% false-positive rate, evaluated against 80,000 synthetic chat excerpts, distinguishing private individuals from public figures, and accepted that it "can significantly reduce risk." By contrast, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4-era filtering removed "only a subset of personal identifiers" and "did not detect opinions, sensitive characteristics linked to identifiable persons," and did not exclude social media or children's sites [V, ¶88, ¶103, ¶108-114]. A clearance business that cannot demonstrate comparable numbers has a documented regulatory yardstick working against it [I]. And because the improved tool was not applied retroactively and OpenAI committed to deprecate GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, model deprecation is emerging as the de facto Canadian remedy for a tainted training corpus, which is an extreme cost and a strong argument for clearing data before training [I, ¶113-114].

Enforcement teeth are the current weak point federally. The matter was recorded as "well-founded and conditionally resolved" with no penalty [V, ¶115-116], so today's federal enforcement risk is reputational and remedial rather than financial [I; the statutory absence of AMP power under PIPEDA is widely understood but was not independently verified here, U]. OpenAI's jurisdictional objection was rejected, so foreign labs cannot escape Canadian jurisdiction by being foreign [V, ¶22-43].

AIDA is dead and nothing replaced it

Bill C-27, and with it AIDA, died on the order paper when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025 [V, DLA Piper, 17 June 2026]. Canada has no comprehensive AI statute in force or before Parliament as of September 2026 [V, Chambers *Artificial Intelligence 2026, Canada*]. For a data broker, the practical effect is that there is no AI-specific compliance layer to clear in Canada: the entire legal test for AI training data is privacy law plus copyright [I].

What exists instead: Bill C-36, the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, introduced at first reading on 15 June 2026 by Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation Evan Solomon, sitting at second reading with no activity recorded as of 29 September 2026, so PIPEDA remains operative [V, LEGISinfo 45-1/C-36; McCarthy Tétrault, 16 June 2026]. AI regulation was deliberately unbundled from privacy: "Bill C-36 abandons standalone AI legislation, addressing AI governance through privacy law, online safety legislation, and sector-specific measures instead" [V, DLA Piper]. C-36 would make express consent the default, expand consent exceptions to cover "business activities, research and de-identification," require documented privacy impact assessments before invoking exceptions, codify that "de-identified personal information does not cease to be personal information," prohibit re-identification with narrow exceptions, and require cross-border transfer risk assessments framed around data as a "strategic national asset." A new Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada, established by Bill C-34 (introduced 10 June 2026), would have binding order authority, with proposed AMPs up to the greater of $10 million or 3% of gross annual global revenue and criminal fines on indictment up to the greater of $25 million or 5% [V, DLA Piper 17 June 2026; ISED news release, 15 June 2026].

C-36's "research and de-identification" consent exception, if enacted as described, would be the single most commercially significant change for a clearance business, because it is the closest thing Canada would have to a lawful basis for processing personal data into training-ready datasets without consent. Its scope will be set largely by regulations that do not exist [I]. But given that the bill sat at second reading with no recorded activity for over three months, and that this is the third attempt in six years after C-11 and C-27 both died, passage before 2027 is not assured [I]. Whether C-36 contains any provision specifically addressing AI training data, data brokers, or the sale of personal information was checked against three separate sources and none identified one [U].

Quebec is the binding jurisdiction

For a data business, Quebec matters more than PIPEDA, and by a wide margin. All section text below is from the consolidated English text at LégisQuébec, CQLR c. P-39.1, retrieved 29 September 2026 [V].

Consent under s. 14 "must be clear, free and informed and be given for specific purposes... requested for each such purpose, in clear and simple language," presented separately from other information, valid "only for the time necessary," and consent not given in accordance with the Act "is without effect." Section 12 blocks secondary use within the enterprise without consent, requires *express* consent for sensitive information, and permits five exceptions including use "necessary for study or research purposes or for the production of statistics and if the information is de-identified." The "consistent purpose" test is narrow and expressly excludes marketing: "commercial or philanthropic prospection may not be considered a consistent purpose." Section 13 requires express consent for disclosure of sensitive information to third parties.

Section 17 is the sharpest operational constraint on any Canada-to-US AI data pipeline. Before communicating personal information outside Québec, an enterprise must conduct a privacy impact assessment weighing sensitivity, purposes, protection measures including contractual ones, and "the legal framework applicable in the State in which the information would be communicated." The transfer may proceed only if the assessment "establishes that it would receive adequate protection," and it "must be the subject of a written agreement." Note that this applies to transfers outside Québec, including to the rest of Canada, not only outside the country. It requires a documented finding of adequacy about US law, and US law's treatment of foreign personal data is precisely the thing being assessed; unlike the EU, Quebec has published no adequacy list [I; the absence of a list is U].

Section 23 sets Canada's only statutory anonymization standard: information is anonymized "if it is, at all times, reasonably foreseeable in the circumstances that it irreversibly no longer allows the person to be identified directly or indirectly," achieved "according to generally accepted best practices and according to the criteria and terms determined by regulation." The Anonymization Regulation, published in the *Gazette officielle du Québec* on 15 May 2024, requires anonymization "carried out under the supervision of a person qualified in the field," assesses effectiveness against three criteria (individualization, correlation and inference), and requires a register of anonymizations [V, McCarthy Tétrault and Fasken bulletins, May 2024]. Quebec's *de-identified* standard is much weaker (s. 12: no longer allows the person to be *directly* identified), and the two terms are legally distinct and not interchangeable.

Quebec treats anonymization as a process with an audit trail, not a data state. A broker selling "anonymized" datasets into Quebec needs the qualified-person supervision, the three-criteria assessment and the register, or it cannot substantiate the claim [I]. And the cost of that process is high enough that it functions as a barrier to entry favouring larger, better-capitalized intermediaries [I].

The penalty structure is what makes Quebec bite. AMPs (ss. 90.1, 90.12) reach $50,000 for a natural person and otherwise $10,000,000 or, if greater, 2% of worldwide turnover. Penal offences (s. 91) range from $5,000-$100,000 for a natural person and $15,000-$25,000,000, or if greater 4% of worldwide turnover, for others, and s. 91(5) makes it an offence to identify or attempt to identify a natural person "using de-identified information without the authorization of the person holding the information or using anonymized information." Directors who ordered, authorized or consented are parties to the offence (s. 93). And s. 93.1 guarantees punitive damages of not less than $1,000 where an intentional or grossly negligent infringement causes injury, which makes class actions economically viable without proof of quantified loss [I]. Quebec's CAI is an active AI regulator, not a theoretical one: it was a full co-investigator in the OpenAI matter [V, #2026-002].

Is "data broker" a regulated category in Canada? Essentially no. There is no Canadian equivalent to a US-state-style data broker registry. The one registration regime is Quebec's "personal information agent" under s. 70, and it is narrower than "data broker": it captures those who "on a commercial basis... establish files on other persons and prepare and communicate to third parties credit reports bearing on the character, reputation or solvency" of those persons. Registered agents face accuracy duties (s. 71), access and rectification procedures (s. 78), mandatory public website disclosure (s. 79), and a hard seven-year destruction rule (s. 79.1), the only hard retention ceiling found anywhere in Canadian private-sector privacy law. Registration confers no endorsement: "A personal information agent may not invoke registration with the Commission to claim that the agent's competence, solvency, conduct or operations are recognized or approved" (s. 70.1) [V, all from LégisQuébec P-39.1]. A firm acquiring and reselling data for AI training would generally not be a personal information agent, so there is no licence to operate as an AI-data broker anywhere in Canada, which means no registration barrier to entry but also no registration-based safe harbour. Market credibility has to be built on demonstrable provenance practice [I]. Provincial consumer-reporting statutes (Ontario's *Consumer Reporting Act* and its equivalents) are the other place a data broker can be a registered category and were not researched [U].

Alberta, BC, Nova Scotia and the residency patchwork

Both PIPA-BC and PIPA-AB apply the same reasonableness gate as PIPEDA s. 5(3), and both were applied concurrently against OpenAI. A federal exemption order exists for BC, but it did not oust OIPC-BC's concurrent jurisdiction in a joint investigation [V, #2026-002, ¶34-40, ¶59]. The operative compliance reality for a national broker is not "which statute applies" but that all four apply at once to the same corpus, and the most restrictive provision governs [I]. Detailed textual comparison of the three statutes was not completed [U].

On public-sector residency: BC liberalized in 2021. Bill 22 removed FOIPPA's general prohibition on disclosure, storage or access outside Canada; s. 33.1 now permits disclosure outside Canada "in accordance with the regulations," with an assessment required where *sensitive* personal information is stored outside Canada [V, BC FOIPPA Manual s. 33.1]. Nova Scotia did not. PIIDPA s. 5(1) requires that personal information in a public body's custody "is stored only in Canada and accessed only in Canada," subject to consent, permitted disclosures, or a head-of-public-body allowance "to meet the necessary requirements of the public body's operation," and s. 2(3) makes the Act paramount over conflicting enactments [V, NS PIIDPA]. The practical consequence is a bifurcated market: private-sector Canadian data can flow to US labs with assessments and contracts, but public-sector-sourced data (health, education, municipal, provincial administrative) is largely closed to a US-processing model in Nova Scotia and requires sensitive-data assessments in BC [I].

Copyright: no exception, no precedent, and two live cases

Canada has no text and data mining exception in the Copyright Act. ISED's consultation ran 12 October 2023 to 15 January 2024 and produced a *What We Heard* report on 11 February 2025 that confirmed "Canada's Copyright Act does not currently contain a specific text and data mining exception" and that "Canadian courts have not yet commented specifically on these questions." Submissions split sharply: cultural creators and collective societies opposed any new exception and favoured licensing, including a preference for "voluntary or opt-in licensing" over opt-out, while the technology sector wanted a clarification that TDM "does not infringe copyright." The government committed only to "consider options." No legislation followed [V, ISED What We Heard report, 11 Feb 2025].

Meanwhile the question is being litigated in two places. A coalition of CBC/Radio-Canada, Postmedia, Metroland, Toronto Star, The Globe and Mail and The Canadian Press sued OpenAI in the Ontario Superior Court on 29 November 2024, seeking punitive damages and disgorgement [V, CBC]. On 7 November 2025 the court ruled the claims can proceed in Ontario, reasoning that "the fair and efficient working of the legal system in Canada favours allowing Canadian authors of Canadian-created Works with claims against foreign companies for breaches of Canadian copyright and other laws to pursue those claims here." The ruling does not address the merits [V, Carters; CBC]. Combined with the Cohere case in SDNY, the configuration is the worst possible one for a data broker: no safe harbour to rely on, jurisdiction confirmed over foreign labs, and no precedent establishing that training is fair dealing. The entire copyright layer of a clearance business must be built on contract, not statute [I]. Because ISED's report records stakeholder support for training-data recordkeeping obligations, provenance documentation is the capability most likely to become a statutory requirement, and is the one a clearance business should build for [I].

Health privacy: permissive statute, prohibitive incentives

PHIPA and its provincial equivalents do not prohibit commercial use of properly de-identified health data. Custodians may de-identify PHI without patient consent, provided they comply with PHIPA's general requirements, and recommended practice is to describe routine de-identification in a public privacy statement and impose contractual obligations on recipients [V, Fasken analysis of PHIPA via search snippet; the ontario.ca e-Laws fetch failed, so the statutory quotes are second-hand]. PHIPA also prohibits re-identification with limited exceptions.

The binding constraint is the reasonable-foreseeability standard, not a prohibition. PHIPA's definition of de-identify turns on whether it is "reasonably foreseeable in the circumstances that it could be utilized, either alone or with other information, to identify the individual." That is a moving target that gets harder to satisfy as external datasets proliferate, so a custodian cannot certify compliance in advance against an unknown future linkage attack, and counsel advises against release [I]. Liability sits entirely with the custodian and benefit sits with the licensee: the hospital must de-identify correctly, publish practices and impose contractual obligations, carrying the regulatory and reputational downside, while the AI company carries the upside. This asymmetry, not the statute, is why "not sharing data can become a default" [I, from the structure plus Boyer's quote].

The proof that this is institutional rather than statutory is that the enabling paperwork was invented this year. INOVAIT, the federally funded image-guided therapy and AI network hosted by Sunnybrook Research Institute, published its Data Licensing Toolkit Version 1.0 on or about 5 January 2026, to "support Canadian healthcare institutions in establishing governance processes for responsible and trustworthy sharing of de-identified health data with industry." Its core instrument is a Data Request Evaluation Scorecard validated and used by the Data Access Committee at Sunnybrook, and it is free to hospitals and not-for-profits under a non-exclusive licensing agreement. The explicit blocker it names: "the health data these companies need most often resides with Canadian healthcare institutions that are reluctant to share data with commercial partners due to caution stemming from a lack of clarity in privacy legislation" [V, INOVAIT and Businesswire release 20260105935834, 5 Jan 2026; the Businesswire page returned 403 so this rests on the snippet plus the fetched INOVAIT page]. A market whose standard contract templates are a nine-month-old free public good is a pre-market [I]. INOVAIT's own framework further limits licensing to "public-good opportunities" and is built on transparency, patient and public representation, defined governance, and Indigenous data sovereignty rights [V, inovait.ca/data], which signals that the emerging Canadian norm is access for collaborative development, not sale of a corpus [I].

Quebec is the most restrictive jurisdiction for commercial secondary use of health data given Law 25's express-consent requirement for sensitive data, with de-identified research as the narrow carve-out [I, verify against the statute]. And the ten-plus distinct provincial regimes make a pan-Canadian health licence structurally expensive: a buyer wanting national-scale data must satisfy PHIPA, Alberta's HIA, Quebec Law 25, BC's FIPPA and PIPA and the rest separately, with different consent standards. That transaction cost alone would deter most buyers even if every custodian were willing [I].

Canada's own diagnosis says the same thing. The Pan-Canadian Health Data Strategy Expert Advisory Group Report 3, chaired by Dr. Vivek Goel and published 3 May 2022 [STALE at 4 years, but still the canonical diagnosis], conceded there is "no single organization mandated to provide stewardship" and "no coherent leadership across jurisdictions," recommended a Health Information Stewardship Council and Chief Health Data Stewards, and estimated the required federal investment at approximately $1 billion annually sustained over 10+ years. Its conclusion: "The fundamental challenge we face is not one of technology, rather it is one of culture," requiring the overcoming of a "legacy of mistrust between jurisdictions" [V, canada.ca PCHDS Report 3]. The recommended Stewardship Council does not appear to have been created [I, from September 2026 commentary still describing governance as an open question]. The EU took the opposite path: the European Health Data Space took effect in March 2025, covering 17 data categories across 27 member states with enumerated secondary uses, where Canada published principles [V, Policy Options, Sept 2026]. A licensee needs an enumerated permission, not a principle, and that is the precise legal gap [I].

Two exceptions to all of this are worth flagging. Genomics is the most brokerable Canadian health data category because consent is obtained prospectively and explicitly contemplates commercial use: CMAJ guidance states consent documents "should make clear how the findings will be used for future research or that the data may be used commercially for the development of new diagnostic tests, new drugs or other commercial products, for which participants will not receive any share of profits or compensation" [V, CMAJ 194(44):E1500, 2022, 4 years old]. Add Genome Canada's $96M Genome Applications Partnership Program commercialization mandate [V, genomecanada.ca via search snippet, date not established] and GA4GH-standard interoperability, and genomics has three properties administrative health data lacks entirely [I]. The consent norm that participants receive no share of profits also removes a benefit-sharing negotiation that would otherwise block licensing [I]. The second exception is the AI scribe channel described earlier.

Finally, a demand-side regulatory driver worth noting: Health Canada published Pre-market Guidance for Machine Learning-Enabled Medical Devices on 1 April 2026, introducing the Predetermined Change Control Plan and setting requirements for data quality, training, validation, bias management, performance monitoring and transparency [V, search snippet citing canada.ca, April 2026]. Canada's Drug Agency has separately issued guidance on reporting AI methods used to extract information from unstructured health data to generate real-world evidence, explicitly covering LLMs [V, CDA-AMC, date not established]. A national regulator writing rules for LLM-derived real-world evidence creates compliance-driven demand for traceable, documented, well-governed training data, which raises the relative value of provenanced Canadian datasets against scraped ones [I].

Indigenous data sovereignty is the least negotiable constraint

OCAP, Ownership, Control, Access and Possession, is not statute. It is a First Nations ethics code originating in the First Nations Regional Longitudinal Health Survey, administered by the First Nations Information Governance Centre, and it is a registered trademark. Its force comes through research ethics policy, institutional review, research agreements and community consent [V, TCPS 2 (2022) Chapter 9; FNIGC via University of Saskatchewan]. Important sourcing caveat: fnigc.ca returned HTTP 403 to every automated access attempt across two separate research threads, so all OCAP detail in this report is second-hand and FNIGC's primary materials should be read directly before acting on any of it [flagged limitation].

Canada's tri-council research ethics policy states that OCAP "addresses issues of privacy, intellectual property, data custody and secondary use of data," and that where divergences exist between institutional policy and community codes "they should be addressed and resolved prior to the commencement of the research." Commercialization is expressly flagged as a harm: "Appropriation of collective knowledge, treatment of such knowledge as a commodity to be traded, or making unauthorized adaptations for commercial purposes, may cause offence or harm to communities from which the knowledge originates." Secondary use requires its own community engagement and consent. And critically for any de-identification workflow: "Small Indigenous communities are characterized by dense networks of relationships. As a result, coding individual data is often not sufficient to mask identities, even when data are aggregated" [V, all TCPS 2 (2022) Ch. 9]. Inuit practice uses possession agreements distinct from research agreements, set out in an MOU with the land claim organization, continuing "long after the research is completed" [V, same].

Institutionally this is advancing, not receding. FNIGC has advanced the First Nations Data Governance Strategy focused on "the repatriation of First Nations data sets from federal agencies"; federal agencies continue implementing the UNDRIP Act (2021) and an Indigenous Data Strategy; the 2023-2026 Data Strategy for the Federal Public Service commits to "a whole-of-government approach to the management and sharing of Indigenous data"; and the Native Women's Association of Canada published an Indigenous Data Sovereignty Toolkit in March 2026 [V, IWGIA; CSPS; NWAC].

Three inferences follow. OCAP's practical force in a commercial transaction runs through institutional research ethics approval, the contractual terms of data-sharing and possession agreements that survive the project, and consultation obligations. None of them is privacy legislation, and all three can block a transaction [I]. A de-identification workflow calibrated for a general population will not satisfy either the ethics standard or, plausibly, Quebec's inference criterion, for First Nations community data [I]. And individual consent is insufficient where the data is collective; community-level authorization is contemplated in addition [I].

Two specific exposures are worth naming. Much Canadian resource data is *about* land subject to treaty, title or consultation obligations, so any drill-core, lidar or site-telemetry dataset originating on or near such land carries a governance claim that OCAP is designed to assert. A broker acquiring a distressed operator's dataset would inherit the claim without inheriting the relationships needed to resolve it [I]. And the CDCL's silence on Indigenous data governance across every release reviewed is notable: a $55M+ federal geoscience digitization program spanning ten jurisdictions with no stated Indigenous data-governance framework in its public communications is an unresolved exposure [I, from a negative reading across the NRCan, Newswire, Nunavut News and CDL sources; a framework may exist and simply not appear in press materials]. Separately, no source was found connecting OCAP specifically to industrial or operational data licensing as opposed to health, research and statistical data, where the literature is concentrated. That is a genuine gap, not a finding of no applicability [U].

Finally, the barrier nobody talks about but the CDCL priced exactly: provincial jurisdiction over natural resources fragments every resource dataset across 13 subnational regimes. The federal government needed 10 separate provincial and territorial participation agreements plus a $2.16M side investment just to bring Saskatchewan's collection in, and is offering provinces up to $5M apiece to digitize their own holdings [V, Newswire and Nunavut News, Sept 2026]. Even the federal government must pay provinces to release resource data it does not control, which is the structural reason no single-buyer national Canadian resource dataset can be assembled privately [I].
