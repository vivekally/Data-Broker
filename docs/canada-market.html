The sizing is broken, so use a different method

Syndicated analyst estimates for the 2026 AI training-dataset market range from US$3.4B to US$11.91B, a 3.5x spread on the same nominal category in the same year: Straits Research at US$3.4B, Research and Markets at US$3.87B, Grand View at US$3.9B, Fortune Business Insights at US$4.44B, Business Research Insights at US$7.48B, and Mordor Intelligence at US$11.91B [V, each per its own published report]. Mordor's own two reports differ by roughly 5x depending on whether the label is "training dataset" or "data labeling." Growth estimates cluster around 21.6% to 28.9% CAGR [V, Technavio and others].

None of these should be quoted as authoritative, and the more defensible conclusion is that they are measuring the wrong category. Adding only the reported top-line of the five largest vendors gives roughly US$6B+ in 2026 vendor revenue before counting Turing, Invisible, TELUS Digital, Appen, iMerit, Centific, Prime Intellect and forty-plus smaller vendors, which already exceeds four of the six analyst estimates [I]. The low estimates are consistent with a legacy image and text annotation services business; the high ones are consistent with including RLHF, expert domain data and RL environments [I]. For an investor-grade picture, use the bottom-up vendor-revenue method and present the analyst range as evidence of measurement failure rather than as a sizing [I]. A separate bottom-up industry estimate puts annual industry revenue at ~US$8.5B with a "static labeling" core of ~US$5B growing 50%+ annually [V as reported, Pebblous, 2026; vendor blog, directional only].

One methodological warning that matters more than any single number: headline "ARR" in this category is systematically inflated by roughly 2.5 to 3x versus net revenue for the marketplace models, because contractors receive 60-70% of top line. Scale AI Inc. and Surge run services P&Ls rather than marketplace take rates, so a comparison table that puts Mercor's $2B next to Scale's $1B is comparing gross to net [I, from the pass-through documented at both Mercor and micro1].

Three of the four leaders are ex-recruiting marketplaces, not ex-annotation firms. Mercor, Handshake and micro1 all pivoted from talent-matching into data supply in 2024-25. The winning asset in 2026 is a vetted expert supply pool plus a matching engine, not an annotation tool or a BPO workforce, and this is the single most important structural fact for anyone scoping a build [I].

Appen's collapse proves two distinct things and they should not be conflated. The first is customer concentration: one customer at 26% of gross revenue, terminable without notice. The second is commodity margin erosion: the Appen Global segment is still shrinking 37% YoY in 2026, two years after the Google loss, while the China segment grows. That cannot be explained by the Google contract [I]. The surviving Appen business is a low-cost-geography business, which is the clearest available signal that commodity annotation is a cost-of-labour game that high-wage geographies lose [I].

Where the capital is going, and where it is not

Capital is flowing hard into expert-marketplace and RL-environment supply and not at all into commodity annotation tooling. The verified disclosed rounds from roughly February 2025 to September 2026 total well over US$1.1B into human-data and environment vendors [V, per-round sources above; this is a floor, not a total, since Crunchbase and PitchBook aggregate views are paywalled]. The bifurcation is the story: the top ten data-labeling *software* companies have raised US$271M between them, ever, while a single expert-marketplace round (Mercor's Series C) was US$350M [V, Seedtable].

Valuations are running at extreme multiples of *net* revenue. Mercor at US$10B on roughly US$400-600M annualized net is 17-25x; at the rumoured US$20B it is 33-50x. These are software multiples applied to a business with a 30-40% take rate and human variable cost [I]. And the Meta-Scale deal appears to have accelerated funding into challengers rather than chilling it: Mercor's US$10B round (Oct 2025), Invisible's round (Sept 2025) and Surge's first-ever raise (July 2025) all cluster in the four months after the June 2025 deal [I, from the date sequence].

On margins, the honest picture has two completely different structures wearing the same label. Reusable, off-the-shelf and synthetic data reaches 80-90% gross margin because a single dataset is sold to multiple customers [V, TechCrunch, 20 Aug 2026, the highest-quality margin source found]. Marketplace take rate is 30-40% before opex [V, Sacra on Mercor; TechCrunch on micro1]. Scale AI Inc. is reported to monetize per task at 50%+ gross margin [V as reported, HeroHunt, low source quality]. Meanwhile commodity labeling wages have roughly halved since 2022 and foundation models now correctly pre-label 60-90% of common cases, leaving humans the hard edges [V as reported, HeroHunt, 2026, low source quality but directionally consistent with Appen's filings]. The premium segments that still hold price are medical imaging, 3D LiDAR and legal document annotation [V as reported, same source]. No audited gross margin exists for any of Scale, Surge, Mercor, Handshake, Turing, Invisible or micro1 [U].

The commoditization argument, honestly assessed

The evidence is genuinely mixed and the honest answer is that the bottom of the market is commoditizing fast and the top is not, yet.

For commoditization: models pre-label most common cases; wages have halved; the vendors themselves are automating humans out (micro1 is increasingly generating synthetic data without human involvement and selling single datasets to multiple customers at 80-90% margin); labs demonstrably can and do terminate; every major lab is building environment teams in-house while buying; and before Scale AI Inc. existed, labelling was done in-house, so the outsourced model is itself recent and reversible [V, per sources above].

Against: lab spend is rising; Mercor grew gross revenue 70% YoY into H1 2026 and Handshake's AI line grew 349% YoY to April 2026, and if labs were internalising, the challengers' growth would be decelerating rather than accelerating [I]; internal teams reportedly "struggle to match the tooling breadth and compliance certifications achieved by specialized vendors"; and enterprises are "unlikely to share proprietary workflow data directly with AI labs," which is the structural reason an intermediary exists at all [V, Sacra on Mercor].

The strongest single correction to the conventional narrative: the "labs said they would source data themselves" claim is not supported as a general trend. The three documented pullbacks (Google, OpenAI, Microsoft) were all from *one* vendor, Scale AI Inc., all within weeks of *one* event, Meta's 49% stake, and the spend moved to Surge, Mercor and Handshake, which is why those three grew triple-digit in the same window. That was vendor substitution driven by a conflict of interest, not disintermediation [I]. The real commoditization risk is to the task type, not to the category: routine annotation is being automated, while expert judgment is what buyers pay escalating prices for [I].

Acquisitions, and one correction that must not propagate

There have been exactly two structurally important transactions, and only one was an acquisition.

Meta's US$14.3B for 49% of Scale AI Inc. (June 2025) reshaped the market not because of the price but because it made a shared supplier untrustworthy and split the category [I]. Before June 2025 a single vendor could serve every lab; after it, ownership by any lab or hyperscaler is disqualifying. That is the structural opening Surge, Mercor, Handshake and micro1 all walked through in the following twelve months [I]. Strategic neutrality is now a priced asset: Surge explicitly markets having no Big Tech ownership and micro1 markets that it does not sell to Chinese model makers. Provenance and alignment have become product features, not compliance overhead [I].

The genuine M&A that followed is small and strategic. EXL (NASDAQ: EXLS) completed its acquisition of iMerit on 3 August 2026, described as "a recognized leader in AI model training, evaluation and reinforcement learning," for up to US$310M: US$170M upfront plus US$140M in earnouts and incentives [V, EXL newsroom; StockTitan]. Over half the consideration is contingent, so the buyer did not underwrite the revenue [I].

CORRECTION TO CARRY FORWARD: a widely-surfaced claim that "EXL acquired Sama in August 2026" is FALSE. The target was iMerit, not Sama. This was checked directly against EXL's own newsroom [V]. The claim appears in search results and must not be allowed to propagate.

Two inferences on exit shape matter for anyone scoping a build. The buyer set for a training-data company is services roll-ups, not labs, because labs now have strong reason not to own a data vendor: owning one poisons it for everyone else, which is exactly what happened to Scale AI Inc. [I]. A data brokerage's realistic exit is a strategic services buyer at a services multiple, not a lab at a software multiple [I]. And the iMerit price is a sobering comparable: a recognized category leader cleared at US$170M cash, against Mercor at US$10B, a 60x gap between the marketplace narrative and the services reality [I].

The Canadian money picture

No Canadian pure-play AI data-supply company was found to have raised a meaningful venture round. The Canadian presence is corporate, not venture-backed: TELUS bought into the category via Lionbridge AI and has now absorbed it; LXT in Toronto grew without disclosed institutional capital. No Canadian company appears in any of the 2025-26 funding lists compiled from Sacra, TechCrunch, Troveo, Pebblous or Seedtable [I, from absence across all of them]. The TELUS take-private is a mildly negative signal: a Canadian-listed company with a billion-dollar annotation asset was worth more inside a telco than in public markets, and the US$539M paid for the minority sits against the US$935M paid for Lionbridge AI alone in 2020 [I].

Canadian capital is category-aware and is not finding Canadian supply-side companies to fund. Radical Ventures announced the Radical Breakouts Fund on ~16 September 2026, the largest VC fund in Canadian history, targeting US$1.4B for AI scale-ups with well over US$1B committed at first close by PSP Investments, CPP Investments, HOOPP, TD Bank Group, BMO Financial Group, CI Global Asset Management and OPTrust [V, T-Net; BetaKit]. Its data-infrastructure bet went to Prime Intellect. Its Canadian portfolio is Cohere and Waabi, i.e. model and application layer. On Cohere's own valuation, note an unresolved conflict: US$500M at US$6.8B in 2025 [V, Data Center Dynamics] versus a reported US$500M Series E led by PSP Investments at US$5.5B in June 2026 [V as reported, Tech Insider Canada]. These are inconsistent, a lower valuation later; do not assert the June 2026 figure [U].

Canadian public money is compute-and-research shaped and contains no data-supply line item anywhere. The June 2026 "AI for All" strategy, announced 4 June 2026 by Prime Minister Carney, totals C$2.3B: Compute Access Fund C$700M, Canadian Tech Growth Fund C$500M, Regional AI Initiative C$500M, AI Missions Program C$200M with a flagship health mission, commercialization via the national institutes C$130M, Canadian AI Safety Institute C$50M, Creative Technology Program C$50M, plus an unspecified CIFAR AI Chairs expansion [V, BetaKit, June 2026]. The strategy contains no funding explicitly designated for data supply, annotation, datasets or data infrastructure [V, absence confirmed on fetch]. The same is true of Phase 2 of the Pan-Canadian AI Strategy (C$443M over ten years, of which C$208M to CIFAR, C$60M to Amii, Mila and Vector for commercialization, C$125M to the Global Innovation Clusters) [V, ISED; CIFAR], and of Scale AI (Canada)'s project funding, whose most recent documented round was C$31.7M across 23 Québec-led AI projects in July 2025 (~C$100M total with private match) with no mention of data supply, datasets or annotation [V, BetaKit, July 2025, absence confirmed on fetch].

A Canadian data-supply venture would be applying to programs that were not designed for it. Scale AI (Canada) funds adoption projects by industrial consortia (a retailer plus a lab plus an AI vendor), not vendor capacity-building, so a data business would have to structure itself as a consortium partner on someone else's supply-chain project to access that money [I]. The plausible remaining routes are NRC IRAP, SR&ED, and the C$500M Canadian Tech Growth Fund as equity, and no figures could be sourced for any of them [U].

One notable contrast: China's state plan targets, by end of 2026, "industrial data consortia... trusted interconnection platforms for key industries... and high-quality, standardized and tradable industrial datasets," plus "industrial data production and training bases" [V as a statement of stated policy, Science and Technology Daily, PRC state outlet, March 2026]. Canada has announced no comparable tradable-industrial-dataset policy [U, no equivalent found].

Where the gaps are real, and the steelman for walking away

Everything above is reporting. This section is analysis, and it is separated deliberately.

The gaps that are genuine

Winter and adverse-weather driving data is a genuinely scarce global asset, and Canada is one of very few jurisdictions that can generate it at scale in dense urban conditions. The Canadian Adverse Driving Conditions dataset is described by its authors as the first autonomous driving dataset focused specifically on adverse conditions: 7,000 annotated frames from 8 cameras, a VLP-32C lidar and a Novatel GNSS+INS, across 75 driving sequences covering over 20 km, collected over 3 days in March 2018 and February 2019, produced by Waterloo's WISE Lab and Toronto's TRAIL Lab [V, arXiv:2001.10117; IJRR 2021]. Toronto's Boreas dataset offers over 350 km across a full year of seasonal variation including falling snow, making it Canada's most substantial winter AV asset on distance [V, arXiv:2203.10168]. The competing datasets are both small, US and non-commercially licensed: WADS (Michigan, 20 scenes) and Ithaca365 (Cornell, 15 km route), both CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 [V, SPIE; Ithaca365]. CADC is the single most commercially accessible Canadian AV dataset because it offers a commercial-licence pathway rather than a flat non-commercial bar, and nobody is documented as ever having taken that licence [V that the arrangement is stated, via Segments.ai; the official host cadcd.uwaterloo.ca refused an HTTPS connection during research, which is itself relevant to whether the pathway is live].

But the edge is entirely latent, and it is being captured by someone else. At 7,000 frames and 20 km, CADC's commercial value is as a benchmark and validation set, not a training corpus [I]. The largest collector of labelled, sensor-complete, adverse-weather Canadian edge cases through 2026 will be Gatik, a US company running 50 trucks through GTA winters with no obligation to share anything beyond confidential regulatory reporting [I]. Meanwhile Ontario's ACMV pilot, which runs 1 August 2025 to 1 August 2035 and mandates daily recording of video, sensor data, weather, GPS timestamps at intervals not exceeding one minute, driver interventions and automated system status, with three-year retention, monthly trip records and annual reporting of disengagements, hard braking and safety-critical events, will accumulate one of the richest government-held longitudinal records of commercial AV operation anywhere [V, ontario.ca ACMV pilot conditions]. And it is structurally unavailable: "MTO will treat all data from individual carriers as confidential," with only aggregate and non-commercially-sensitive data shareable with municipalities or Transport Canada [V, same]. The carrier owns the data, the regulator holds a confidential copy it cannot commercialise, and there is therefore no supply side for a Canadian AV data market, which explains the absence of a demand side better than any claim about buyer interest [I].

Sanctuary AI's tactile-plus-teleoperation corpus is the most globally differentiated robot-learning data asset in Canada, because visual-tactile paired demonstration data is rarer than vision-only demonstration data and Sanctuary's hand hardware is unusually instrumented [I]. Set that against the fact that XDOF raised US$70M to build robot-data pipelines, and AI labs are already paying it to collect robot training data [V, TechCrunch, 17 June 2026], and there is a real, identifiable arbitrage sitting unexploited: a paying global market and a Canadian producer of a scarcer variant of the same data, with no observed transaction between them [I]. No source establishes that Sanctuary has ever sold or licensed data [U].

The distressed-acquisition channel is proven but unexploited. Canada's CCAA process is a functioning, court-supervised mechanism for acquiring data-bearing assets cheaply, and UrtheCast proves it works. But the pricing precedent cuts both ways: Deimos sold for US$3.2M and Farmers Edge's public equity for cents, which implies the market currently values these datasets at near zero when detached from a going-concern customer relationship. A brokerage buying at those prices is buying cheap; a brokerage trying to resell at meaningful prices faces the same market that produced those valuations [I]. Farmers Edge is the specific asset most worth diligencing: a multi-country, multi-million-acre agronomic sensor dataset now sitting inside an insurance holding company with no obvious AI-training monetization motive [I]. What Fairfax has done with it since 2024 is a live, checkable question with no evidence found [U]. No Canadian distressed-asset buyer, fund or advisory was identified as specifically targeting data assets [U].

Earth observation is where a Canadian data brokerage would find an actual functioning market with pricing precedent, because SkyWatch already proves API-mediated third-party EO resale works commercially in Canada [I]. And subsurface and remote-sensing data is the realistic Canadian entry point for any industrial data business, because provenance, format standards (PPDM) and an existing willingness to pay all already exist there, whereas for factory or mine-site telemetry none of the three does [I]. Note though that whether any Canadian EO provider has licensed imagery specifically for foundation-model or geospatial-model training: no evidence found [U].

Genomics is the most brokerable Canadian health data category, for the three reasons set out in the regulatory section, and it should be contrasted sharply with administrative health data, which has none of those properties [I].

One important nuance on point 11, because it works both ways. Simulation-first architecture increases, not decreases, the marginal value of high-quality real sensor data, because digital twins are reconstructed from real logs. What it reduces is the need for redundant miles. The residual appetite is for rare, hard, distribution-tail data: adverse weather, unusual agents, near-collisions [I, from the TechCrunch description of digital-twin construction combined with the "smaller physical footprint" characterisation]. A simulation-heavy Canadian AV sector would be a selective premium buyer of edge-case data, not a bulk buyer [I]. And Waabi's simulation-first architecture paired with a scarce real-world winter corpus is, on paper, the strongest strategic pairing in Canadian AV, because simulation is most valuable when seeded with rare real data and winter is the rarest. No source indicates this pairing is being pursued as a data strategy [I].

What would have to be true for the counter-case to fail

These are the falsifiable conditions, stated so they can be tested rather than argued [I throughout]. The venture sells expert judgment in a regulated vertical, not annotation, so global wage arbitrage does not apply. It owns an exclusive or high-switching-cost supply relationship, such as a professional body, a licensing regime or an institutional partnership, rather than a contractor pool anyone can poach. It sells provenance and jurisdictional attestation as the product, which is the one thing the Meta-Scale episode proved buyers will switch over. Its first customers are enterprises with data they will not give a lab directly, not the six frontier labs. And it is capitalised to survive a customer loss of 30%+ of revenue with no notice.

Read against the Canadian evidence, that profile points to one shape more than any other: the intersection of categories F and G for regulated sector data, meaning rights-cleared, consent-documented, provenance-auditable Canadian-origin health or industrial data. The supporting conditions are real: ICES-scale health assets, 15+ years of ICD-10, a national regulator writing LLM-derived-RWE guidance, the C.D. Howe diagnosis that Canada "has strong AI research and rich longitudinal health data, but lacks the commercialization architecture" [V, C.D. Howe Institute, publication date not established, verify before quoting], and Private AI and Replica Analytics proving the de-identification and synthesis layer is buildable here. This is an inference, not a finding, and the steelman above remains unrebutted on the demand side [I].
