Canadian AI data broker landscape

Canada holds the data, brokers nothing

The central finding of this research is a negative one, and it is the most valuable thing in this report: there is almost no Canadian AI training-data broker market to compete in. Across mining, oil and gas, forestry, agriculture, rail, ports, robotics, autonomous vehicles and healthcare, not one Canadian operator was found licensing its proprietary operational data to an AI developer as training data [U, searched across trade press, company releases and cluster announcements; absence of evidence, not verified absence]. No Canadian company sells AV perception data, dashcam video, teleoperation logs or robot demonstration data as a product [U, same caveat]. Canadian health data is effectively not brokerable for structural rather than legal reasons: the major stewards grant access to a secure environment rather than possession of data, and the country's first governance toolkit for hospital-to-industry data licensing shipped as a free public good in January 2026 [V, INOVAIT, 5 Jan 2026]. An industry directory sweep of roughly forty RL-environment vendors found zero Canadian headquarters [V, RL List 2026]. No Canadian pure-play AI data-supply company was found to have raised a venture round, while Toronto's Radical Ventures put its data-infrastructure money into a non-Canadian company [V, PYMNTS, 2026]. What Canada does have is a resource position rather than an intermediary position: one globally significant labelling operator in Vancouver, a deep bench of first-party sector-data holders in energy, geospatial and health, a credible privacy and de-identification layer, and a federal government spending public money to build the one deliberately AI-ready industrial corpus in the country. The gap between what Canada owns and what Canada sells is the whole story, and it is wide enough to be either the opportunity or the warning, depending on how you read the rest of this report.

Two different companies are called Scale AI, and only one of them is Canadian

Before any figure in this report is read, this collision has to be cleared, because it corrupts search results, database listings and market maps alike.

Scale AI (Canada) is properly *Canada's AI-Powered Supply Chains Cluster*, a Montréal-headquartered federal Global Innovation Cluster launched in 2018 at scaleai.ca. It is a grant-maker. It reimburses up to 40% of eligible project expenses, and up to 50% for qualifying Québec expenditures, on 12 to 18 month AI-adoption projects [V, scaleai.ca projects]. It is funded with C$284M from the Government of Canada and C$53M from the Government of Québec, matched by private contributions [V, scaleai.ca]; the launch announcement in 2018 was framed as CAD 230 million in matched federal funding [V, ISED; Research Money].

Scale AI, Inc. is a San Francisco data-labelling and RL-environment company at scale.com, with roughly 300,000 contractors, US$870M of 2024 revenue, and a US$29B valuation after Meta bought 49% of it for US$14.3B in June 2025 [V, Sacra; Forbes, 14 May 2026].

They have no relationship whatsoever. Every search for "Scale AI Canada" returns the cluster. A Tracxn "investments by Scale AI" listing surfaced during this research and almost certainly conflates the two [I, from the incompatible business models]. Throughout this report the Montréal cluster is written as Scale AI (Canada) and the US company as Scale AI Inc. There is one place where both appear in the same sentence and it matters: Scale AI Inc. supplied the lidar ground-truth annotations for the Canadian Adverse Driving Conditions dataset produced by the University of Waterloo and the University of Toronto [V, arXiv:2001.10117]. That is a US vendor selling services *into* a Canadian academic dataset, which is exactly the direction of flow this report keeps finding.

Conclusion

The most useful way to hold this landscape is not as a market with gaps but as an hourglass with an empty middle. Canada is thick at the bottom, where the data is produced: methane plumes over the oil sands, drill core from ten jurisdictions, 13.4 million Ontarians' longitudinal health records, minute-resolution telemetry from autonomous trucks in Toronto winters, tactile demonstrations from humanoid hands in Vancouver. It is thick at the top, where models are built and where one Vancouver company processes two billion labels a year. And it is almost empty in the layer that turns raw sector data into lab-ready training assets, which is precisely the layer that captures the most spend per employee anywhere in the world. That emptiness is not an oversight waiting to be corrected. It is produced by four forces acting together: no domestic frontier-lab buyer to embed with, a wage structure that loses the commodity fight before it starts, provincial jurisdiction that fragments every national dataset into thirteen negotiations, and a liability structure in which the custodian carries all the risk while the licensee captures all the upside.

Two things follow that were not obvious going in. The first is that the single most important regulatory development in this space is not an AI law at all, it is a thirty-year-old privacy statute and a regulation drafted in 2000. AIDA is dead, no AI act is coming, and the entire legal test for Canadian training data is PIPEDA section 5(3) plus copyright contract. Four regulators used that section in May 2026 to find a frontier lab's collection unlawful independent of consent, and SOR/2001-7's five closed categories foreclose the "it's public" defence by their text. Any Canadian data business is therefore competing on provenance discipline, not on legal innovation, and the good news buried in that is that provenance discipline is exactly the thing the Meta-Scale episode proved buyers will switch vendors over, and the thing nobody in the top four actually sells as an auditable product. The second is that Canadian data has been leaving the country steadily and profitably for six years, and not once through a data licence. It left through Ocado's US$262M, Rockwell's US$550M, Aetion's acquisition of Replica, ALS's purchase of GoldSpot, Enverus's US$100M for a well-log library with no Canadian party at the table, Lafayette's bankruptcy bid for Attabotics, and Gatik's trucks quietly filling US servers with Ontario winter. The equity channel works, the licensing channel does not exist, and anyone building here should understand that they would be inventing the second channel rather than competing in it. That is a much harder thing than entering a market, and it is also the only honest description of what the opportunity actually is.
