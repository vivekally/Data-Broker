Global players with Canadian operations or Canadian data generation

Gatik is the clearest live case. It is a US company [V, Businesswire, 27 Jan 2026] operating a five-year expansion agreement with Loblaw that puts 20 autonomous trucks across the GTA distribution network by end of 2025 and a further 30 by end of 2026, for 50 trucks operating daily in Greater Toronto [V, Loblaw]. In January 2026 its contracts were reported at US$600M [V, Forbes]. Canadian-origin road data, including the winter data discussed below, is accumulating on a US company's servers with no Canadian entity acquiring rights to it, and no data sale is taking place [I].

IQVIA is the closest thing to a commercial health-data intermediary operating in Canada, but it sits in the pharma real-world-evidence channel sourced from pharmacy, claims and EMR data rather than the single-payer channel [I]. It markets Canada as "an attractive location for Real World Evidence studies," citing 15+ years of ICD-10 use in Canadian hospital and emergency settings [V, IQVIA, January 2025, 20 months old]. Its global portfolio claim of "over 1.2 billion de-identified patient records" is a company self-claim with no disclosed Canadian share [V that the claim was made, iqvia.com] [U].

LiveRamp reaches the Canadian market through Bell-owned Environics rather than directly [V, DMN]. Welocalize / Welo Global recruits "AI Data Annotator, French (Canada)" roles, which is direct evidence that Canadian-French linguistic data collection is being commissioned by a foreign localisation vendor [V that the posting exists, Lever; I on the interpretation]. And the US gig platforms (Mercor, Outlier, DataAnnotation, Alignerr) recruit Canadian contractors directly with no Canadian legal entity [V that the claim was published, theaiemploymentagency.com] [U, affiliate blog; no primary platform terms were read].

That last point produces one of the more interesting inferences in this research. Canada's participation in the global human-data supply chain is as an individual labour pool, not as a corporate node [I]. And because the rates available to Canadians through those platforms (Mercor at US$40-100+/hr, DataAnnotation at US$20-65/hr, Outlier at US$14-30/hr generalist) sit in the *high* band of the global annotation workforce [V that the ranges were published, aigigjobs.com] [U on accuracy; treat the ordering as more reliable than the numbers], Canada is structurally uncompetitive for volume labelling and potentially competitive for expert data [I].

The public and quasi-public holders, which are the most consequential Canadian assets

Four institutions hold more relevant data than any Canadian company, and none of them is a seller.

ICES (Ontario), founded 1992, is a prescribed entity under PHIPA with access to administrative health records covering 13.4 million people [V, ices.on.ca; PMC]. Its inventory "is stored on servers housed within a closed computing system at ICES Central," scientists "must apply for and justify each dataset requested," and the main external pathway is the Applied Health Research Question model in which ICES analysts run the query and return results [V, ices.on.ca]. ICES is effectively closed to data egress [I].

CIHI holds national record-level data behind a Secure Access Environment and runs an explicit two-tier commercial price list: data preparation at $190/hour (Price A) versus $380/hour (Price B), and SAE annual access at $3,325 versus $6,650 for up to three users, where Price B covers "private commercial operations, software vendors, consultants" and foreign clients [V, CIHI, fetched Sept 2026; fee-table vintage not dated on the page]. This is the single clearest priced commercial route into Canadian health data, and it is a services fee, not a data licence: it is denominated in analyst hours and per-user seats rather than dataset scope, term or field of use, which is the economic signature of cost recovery [I]. CIHI's public page contains no explicit restriction on AI training [V by absence] but that is an absence, not a permission, and the SAE Agreement text was not retrieved [U].

Population Data BC holds demographic, health, education, social services, justice, workers' compensation and ICBC data, applies the Five Safes framework, and reports managing 345 data access requests or projects at various stages [V, popdata.bc.ca]. Whether private commercial entities are eligible at all in BC was not established [U].

VITAL is the one to watch. Based at St. Michael's Hospital within Unity Health Toronto and rooted in the GEMINI network founded in 2014 by Dr. Fahad Razak and Dr. Amol Verma, it will connect more than 160 hospitals across Ontario, Alberta and Quebec, covering over 20 million Canadians, described as the largest hospital data network in Canada [V, search-snippet sourcing citing unityhealth.to and CBC, June 2026; weaker than a fetched page]. It received $100 million in federal funding under the "AI for All" strategy announced in June 2026, on top of $110M in prior federal, provincial and institutional funding, for over $210 million total [V, same snippet sourcing]. VITAL is the first Canadian institution designed from the outset as an AI-training-data supply layer rather than a research-access layer [I, from its framing as giving health AI developers "a national foundation to build on" and its placement inside a federal AI strategy rather than a health-research program]. Its actual industry access terms, pricing and IP allocation are unknown, and this is the single highest-value open question in the entire body of research [U].

Alongside these sits the Canadian Digital Core Library (CDCL), the most deliberate attempt anywhere in Canada to manufacture an AI-ready industrial corpus. NRCan is investing $40M over two years starting in 2026-27, with $15M allocated to the Creative Destruction Lab to build the national platform and $2.16M to support Saskatchewan's digital core library; 10 provinces and territories have signed participation agreements; and core scanning began on 14 September 2026 in Calgary, starting with Northwest Territories samples [V, Newswire / Government of Canada, Sept 2026]. NRCan is separately offering up to $5 million apiece for provincial and territorial geoscience data projects until 31 March 2028 [V, Nunavut News, 21 Sept 2026]. Crucially, "established core-scanning providers are invited to contribute services and resulting data in kind," which is the closest thing in Canada to an industrial data-pooling mechanism [V, Newswire, Sept 2026; I on the characterisation]. Six mining majors are reported to be participating: Agnico Eagle, Anglo American, BHP, Teck, Vale and Hudbay [V, Giant, Aug 2026] [I, single secondary source; NRCan's own September release does not name them, so treat participation as reported rather than contractual]. Whether CDCL data will be open-licence or commercially licensed is not stated in any source found, and it is the single most commercially important unanswered question about the asset [U].

The demand side, where the honest answer is mostly nobody

This is the section that will disappoint anyone hoping for a clean buyer map, and the disappointment is the finding.

Not one Canadian company was established as selling training data to a named frontier lab. Not in industrial data, not in robotics or AV, not in healthcare [U, across three separate research threads, each with incomplete searches acknowledged]. The closest to a positive signal is TELUS: after taking TELUS Digital private, TELUS reported that the "Digital Solutions pipeline reaccelerated in Q4 [2025], driving 17% YoY revenue growth, landing new AI data projects with hyperscalers" [V, TELUS FY2025 results, Feb 2026]. The hyperscalers are unnamed, and TELUS does not break out AI Data Solutions revenue separately [V, absence confirmed in the filing summary]. The privatization on 31 October 2025 removed the only public disclosure window into the largest Canadian player in this market, which means any future sizing of the Canadian category will be blind to its largest component [I].

Where Canadian data does have paying buyers, those buyers are operators, regulators, insurers and pharma sponsors, not model labs. GHGSat sells methane observations to ExxonMobil and Aramco. MDA sells EO data to nine early customers and to NASA. geoLOGIC sells subscription access to well data to the oil and gas industry. Pentavere sells real-world evidence extraction to Takeda Canada and to the Canadian Personalized Health Innovation Network. Environics sells segmentation to marketers. Reklaim sells consented consumer data to "brands & agencies, platforms, and data companies." Rithmik sells per-asset monitoring subscriptions to mine operators. Canadian rail and port operational data is being consumed by AI vendors under service contracts (Duos, Informatica, Deloitte with AWS, Mely.ai, IVADO Labs) rather than sold by the asset owners: in every arrangement found, the asset owner is the paying customer, not the seller [I, from the structure of every rail and port arrangement identified].

Three specific demand-side observations are worth carrying forward because they are load-bearing.

First, the one non-content operational archive price point found anywhere. Google reportedly bid US$10 million for a Spirit Airlines operational archive [V, Troveo, 23 July 2026; single-source and unusual]. That is the only evidence located that a frontier-lab-class buyer will price a non-media operational dataset at all, and it is worth more attention than its single-source status suggests, because it is the closest analogue to what a Canadian industrial data asset would be selling.

Second, Canada's nearest frontier lab does not appear to buy. Cohere is co-headquartered in Toronto and San Francisco and staffs a standing bench of domain annotators on its own payroll: Data Annotation Specialist roles across Investment Banking and Private Equity, Safety, Competitive Coding, Science, Data Science and Generalist, structured as "part-time contract positions requiring 16 hours per week at a $30/hour contract rate" [V, Cohere Ashby board, 29 Sept 2026]. No supplier-facing intake path, no data-partnerships team title, and no reported data-licensing deal were found [U, a genuine void rather than a negative finding]. Cohere's data posture looks insourced rather than procured, which is a meaningfully different buying profile from US labs that route heavy volume through Surge and Mercor [I]. For a Canadian supplier this is the awkward finding: the nearest lab is the one least evidenced to buy from third parties, and its published $30/hour internal reference rate is a low bar for a fully-loaded Canadian vendor rate to beat [I].

Third, and most underappreciated: AI scribes are probably the largest live channel by which Canadian clinical data reaches commercial AI training. Ambient listening tools capture confidential health information, explicit patient consent is required, and "when patients consent, they agree their data can be used to train the AI model" [V, Policy Options, April 2026]. This is a consent-based, per-encounter flow that bypasses the institutional licensing problem entirely [I]. It merits dedicated follow-up, because it means the formal licensing debate is happening while the informal channel is already operating.

For completeness on the buyers who exist but cannot transact: Canada has more than 80 companies working with health data and AI to improve triaging, remote monitoring and diagnostics [V, search snippet citing techforum.ca coverage of the VITAL announcement, June 2026]. The claim that these 80-plus companies "now have access to a national-scale dataset" through VITAL is promotional framing and the access terms are unspecified; it is unlikely that 80 companies had executed data agreements as of mid-2026 [I]. What they actually encounter is "limited, and often dysfunctional, access to data" [V, Boyer & Roifman, *Healthcare Management Forum*, doi 10.1177/08404704261454317, 2026, via search snippet], and a hospital default of refusal: "industry is often regarded with suspicion or distrust, and in such scenarios, not sharing data can become a default" [V, Torys Q&A with Phil Boyer, fetched Sept 2026]. The binding constraint on the buy side is counterparty willingness and transaction cost, not price. CIHI's Price B at $380/hour and $6,650/year for three SAE seats is trivially affordable for any funded AI company, and the sector still reports dysfunctional access. If price were the constraint, that tier would have solved it [I].
